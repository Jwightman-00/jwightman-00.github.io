State the Lorentz force on a point charge.	\( \mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}) \)<br><br>\(\mathbf{E}\) is force per unit charge; \(\mathbf{B}\) is force per unit current.	eo h1_emwaves
State the four Maxwell equations in vacuum (differential form).	\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \rho_t/\varepsilon_0\)<br>\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\)<br>\(\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t\)<br>\(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J}_t + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t\)<br><br>The last term is the displacement current; the curl equations couple E and B in time-varying situations.	eo h1_emwaves
State Maxwell's equations in a medium (in terms of D and H).	\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho\) (free charge)<br>\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\)<br>\(\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\dot{\mathbf{B}}\)<br>\(\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + \dot{\mathbf{D}}\) (free current)	eo h1_emwaves
Define the auxiliary fields D and H.	\( \mathbf{D} = \varepsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P} \) (electric displacement)<br>\( \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0 - \mathbf{M} \) (magnetic intensity)<br><br>P and M are the polarization and magnetization densities.	eo h1_emwaves
How is polarization P related to bound charges and currents?	Volume bound charge density: \( \rho_p = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P} \)<br>Surface bound charge density: \( \sigma_p = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}} \)<br>Bound current density: \( \mathbf{J}_p = \dot{\mathbf{P}} \)<br><br>From the continuity equation \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}_p = -\dot\rho_p\).	eo h1_emwaves
How is magnetization M related to bound currents?	Volume bound current density: \( \mathbf{J}_m = \nabla\times\mathbf{M} \)<br>Surface bound current density: \( \mathbf{j}_m = \mathbf{M}\times\hat{\mathbf{n}} \)	eo h1_emwaves
State the constitutive relations for linear isotropic media.	\( \mathbf{P} = \varepsilon_0\chi\mathbf{E} \) and \( \mathbf{M} = \chi_m\mathbf{H} \)<br><br>Hence \( \mathbf{D} = \varepsilon\varepsilon_0\mathbf{E} \) and \( \mathbf{B} = \mu\mu_0\mathbf{H} \), where \(\varepsilon = 1+\chi\) is the relative permittivity and \(\mu = 1+\chi_m\) is the relative permeability.	eo h1_emwaves
State the EM energy density.	\( u = \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{D} + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{H} \)	eo h1_emwaves
Define the Poynting vector.	\( \mathbf{N} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H} \)<br><br>It is the energy flux per unit area per unit time carried by the electromagnetic field.	eo h1_emwaves
Show \(dU/dt = -\oint\mathbf{N}\cdot d\mathbf{S} - \int\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\,dV\) in words.	The rate of change of EM energy in a volume equals the inward Poynting flux through its surface minus the work done on free currents inside (Joule dissipation). This is Poynting's theorem.	eo h1_emwaves
Why introduce the vector potential A?	Because \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\) and the divergence of any curl vanishes, B can always be written as \(\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}\). For time-varying B, E is no longer conservative, so a scalar potential alone is insufficient.	eo h1_emwaves
Express E in terms of the potentials A and ϕ.	\( \mathbf{E} = -\dot{\mathbf{A}} - \nabla\phi \)<br><br>From \(\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\dot{\mathbf{B}} = -\nabla\times\dot{\mathbf{A}}\), integrating gives this form (the gradient of any scalar can be added).	eo h1_emwaves
State the four EM boundary conditions at an interface between two media.	Across an interface (no free charges/currents):<br>(1) \(D_\perp\) conserved (from \(\oint\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S}=0\))<br>(2) \(B_\perp\) conserved<br>(3) \(E_\parallel\) conserved<br>(4) \(H_\parallel\) conserved<br><br>Free interface charge breaks (1); free interface current breaks (4).	eo h1_emwaves
Derive the EM wave speed in a medium.	Taking curl of \(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}=-\dot{\mathbf{B}}\) and substituting from the other ME's gives \(\nabla^2\mathbf{E} = \varepsilon\varepsilon_0\mu\mu_0\,\ddot{\mathbf{E}}\), a wave equation with speed:<br><br>\( v = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon\varepsilon_0\mu\mu_0}} = \dfrac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}} = \dfrac{c}{n} \)<br><br>where \(n=\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}\) is the refractive index.	eo h1_emwaves
State the relationships between E, B, k, H for plane EM waves in an isotropic medium.	\(\mathbf{B}\perp\mathbf{k}\) and \(\mathbf{D}\perp\mathbf{k}\); \(\mathbf{B}\parallel\mathbf{H}\) and \(\mathbf{D}\parallel\mathbf{E}\). So \((\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H},\mathbf{k})\) are mutually perpendicular and form a right-handed set with \(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{N}\parallel\mathbf{k}\). These are transverse EM waves.	eo h1_emwaves
Relate |E|/|B| and |E|/|H| for a plane wave.	\( |\mathbf{E}|/|\mathbf{B}| = \omega/k = v = c/n \)<br><br>\( |\mathbf{E}|/|\mathbf{H}| = \sqrt{\mu\mu_0/(\varepsilon\varepsilon_0)} = Z = \sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon}\,Z_0 \)<br><br>where \(Z_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0} \approx 377\,\Omega\) is the impedance of free space.	eo h1_emwaves
When can EM waves be longitudinal?	When free charge is present so \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}\neq 0\), or in the special case \(\varepsilon(\omega)=0\) which allows \(\mathbf{k}\parallel\mathbf{E}\). Plasma waves at \(\omega = \omega_p\) (where \(\varepsilon=0\)) are longitudinal.	eo h1_emwaves
State the Fresnel reflection coefficients for s and p polarisations.	\( r_p = \dfrac{n_2\cos\theta_1 - n_1\cos\theta_2}{n_2\cos\theta_1 + n_1\cos\theta_2} \) (E in plane of incidence)<br><br>\( r_s = \dfrac{n_1\cos\theta_1 - n_2\cos\theta_2}{n_1\cos\theta_1 + n_2\cos\theta_2} \) (E perpendicular)<br><br>p = E in plane of incidence; s = senkrecht (perpendicular).	eo h1_emwaves
State the Brewster angle.	\( \theta_B = \tan^{-1}(n_2/n_1) \)<br><br>At this angle of incidence \(r_p = 0\): light polarized in the plane of incidence is fully transmitted, while s-polarized light is partially reflected. Used as a polarizing device.	eo h1_emwaves
What happens to \(r_p\) and \(r_s\) at grazing incidence?	Both \(r_p, r_s \to -1\) as \(\theta_1 \to \pi/2\). All incident light is reflected, with a phase reversal.	eo h1_emwaves
At normal incidence, what is the relation between \(r_p\) and \(r_s\)?	\( r_p = -r_s \) and \( t_p = t_s \). The minus sign comes from the convention used for which way is 'reflected' for the two polarisations.	eo h1_emwaves
How does superposing two perpendicular plane-polarized waves with real amplitudes give plane polarization?	If \(a_1, a_2\) are real, the x and y components of \(\mathbf{E}_T\) oscillate in phase. Result is a plane-polarized wave at angle \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(a_2/a_1)\) to the x-axis with amplitude \(E_0\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2}\).	eo h1_polarization
How is left-circularly polarized (LCP) light produced from two linear components?	Take \(a_1=1, a_2=i\) (so \(E_y\) lags \(E_x\) by \(\pi/2\)). At fixed z, \(\mathbf{E}_T\) rotates anti-clockwise as seen by an observer towards whom the light propagates. The instantaneous field sweeps out a left-handed helix in space.	eo h1_polarization
What does an observer see for RCP versus LCP light?	The observer (light coming towards them):<br>• LCP: E rotates anti-clockwise<br>• RCP: E rotates clockwise<br><br>Note: electrical engineers use the opposite convention.	eo h1_polarization
State the Jones vectors for x-pol, y-pol, RCP, LCP.	\( \underline{L}_x = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} \), \( \underline{L}_y = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} \)<br><br>\( \underline{C}_R = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix} \), \( \underline{C}_L = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix} \)	eo h1_polarization
Express \(\underline{L}_x\) as a sum of circular polarizations.	\( \underline{L}_x = \dfrac{\underline{C}_R + \underline{C}_L}{\sqrt{2}} \)<br><br>A linearly polarized wave is a superposition of two counter-rotating circularly polarized waves of equal amplitude.	eo h1_polarization
In Jones algebra, what is the order of operations for light passing through optical elements A, B, C?	\( \underline{\underline{J}} = \underline{\underline{J}}_C \cdot \underline{\underline{J}}_B \cdot \underline{\underline{J}}_A \)<br><br>The first element acts first, so its matrix multiplies the input vector first (rightmost in the product).	eo h1_polarization
State the Jones matrix for an x-polarizer.	\( \underline{\underline{J}}_x = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\0 & 0\end{pmatrix} \)<br><br>It transmits only the x-component, blocking the y-component (e.g. a Polaroid film with conducting chains along y).	eo h1_polarization
State the Jones matrix for a polarizer at angle θ to Ox.	\( \underline{\underline{J}}_\theta = \begin{pmatrix}\cos^2\theta & \sin\theta\cos\theta\\\sin\theta\cos\theta & \sin^2\theta\end{pmatrix} \)<br><br>Equivalent to a rotation, projection onto x, then rotation back.	eo h1_polarization
State Malus's law.	For linearly polarized light of intensity \(I_0\) passing through a polarizer at angle \(\theta\) to its polarization direction:<br><br>\( I(\theta) = I_0\cos^2\theta \)<br><br>Falls to zero for crossed polarizers (\(\theta = \pi/2\)).	eo h1_polarization
What is dichroism?	Anisotropic absorption: a dichroic material absorbs light linearly polarized in one direction more than the other. Acts as a polarizer at the molecular level (e.g. Polaroid film with aligned conducting polymer chains).	eo h1_anisotropic
What is birefringence?	Anisotropic refractive index: light of different polarization or different propagation direction experiences a different refractive index. Arises from anisotropic crystal structure (e.g. calcite).	eo h1_anisotropic
Express the dielectric response in an anisotropic medium.	\( D_i = \varepsilon_0 \sum_j \varepsilon_{ij} E_j \)<br><br>The relative permittivity is a second-rank tensor. In general \(\mathbf{D} \not\parallel \mathbf{E}\) in an anisotropic medium.	eo h1_anisotropic
Why is the dielectric tensor Hermitian?	From energy conservation \((\dot{\mathbf{E}}\cdot\mathbf{D} - \mathbf{E}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{D}}) = 0\) for time-harmonic complex fields. Working through gives \(\varepsilon_{ij} = \varepsilon_{ji}^*\), so \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) is Hermitian and can be diagonalised.	eo h1_anisotropic
Define principal axes and principal refractive indices.	For a lossless non-active anisotropic medium, \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) is real symmetric and can be diagonalised in a frame \(\{\hat{e}_1,\hat{e}_2,\hat{e}_3\}\) — the principal axes:<br><br>\( \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \mathrm{diag}(n_1^2, n_2^2, n_3^2) \)<br><br>\(n_1, n_2, n_3\) are the principal refractive indices.	eo h1_anisotropic
Distinguish biaxial, uniaxial, and isotropic crystals.	Biaxial: \(n_1 \neq n_2 \neq n_3\) (e.g. orthorhombic).<br>Uniaxial: two indices equal (e.g. tetragonal calcite, conventionally \(n_1=n_2=n_o\), \(n_3=n_e\)).<br>Isotropic: all three equal (e.g. cubic).	eo h1_anisotropic
Define ordinary and extraordinary refractive indices for a uniaxial crystal.	\(n_o\): refractive index for D perpendicular to the optic axis (\(\hat{e}_3\)).<br>\(n_e\): refractive index for D along the optic axis.<br><br>Birefringence: \(\Delta n = n_e - n_o\). For calcite \(\Delta n = -0.172\) (negative birefringence).	eo h1_anisotropic
When is D parallel to E in an anisotropic medium?	(a) When E lies along a principal axis of the medium.<br>(b) When E is perpendicular to the optic axis of a uniaxial medium (i.e. lies in the \(\hat{e}_1\)-\(\hat{e}_2\) plane where \(\varepsilon_1=\varepsilon_2\)).	eo h1_anisotropic
Why may N not be parallel to k in an anisotropic medium?	In general \(\mathbf{E}\not\parallel\mathbf{D}\), so although D⊥k and H⊥k, \(\mathbf{N}=\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}\) is not parallel to k. The phase propagates along k but the energy propagates along N — they can differ in direction. This is the origin of double refraction.	eo h1_anisotropic
What is the optical indicatrix?	An ellipsoid defined by \( D_x^2/\varepsilon_1 + D_y^2/\varepsilon_2 + D_z^2/\varepsilon_3 = 1 \). The length of its radius vector along a given direction equals the refractive index for a wave with D in that direction.<br><br>So polarization direction (not propagation direction) determines wave velocity.	eo h1_anisotropic
State the formula for the extraordinary refractive index in a uniaxial crystal.	For a wave with k at angle \(\theta\) to the optic axis \(\hat{e}_3\):<br><br>\( \dfrac{(n_b\sin\theta)^2}{n_e^2} + \dfrac{(n_b\cos\theta)^2}{n_o^2} = 1 \)<br><br>where \(n_b\) is the effective refractive index for the extraordinary wavelet.	eo h1_anisotropic
Describe double refraction in calcite.	Light at normal incidence on a calcite crystal (with optic axis at angle to surface normal) splits into two rays: an ordinary ray (D⊥optic axis, spherical wavelets, refracts normally) and an extraordinary ray (D in plane containing optic axis, ellipsoidal wavelets, laterally shifted because N is not along k). Two images of objects viewed through the crystal.	eo h1_anisotropic
State the Jones matrix for a waveplate with fast axis along Ox.	\( \underline{\underline{J}}_{\mathrm{plate}} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\Delta\phi/2} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\Delta\phi/2} \end{pmatrix} \)<br><br>where \(\Delta\phi = \omega(n_s-n_f)d/c\) is the phase retardation between slow and fast axes.	eo h1_waveplates
State the Jones matrix for a quarter-wave plate (fast axis along Ox).	\( \underline{\underline{J}}_{\lambda/4,x} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\0 & i\end{pmatrix} \)<br><br>(after dropping a global phase). Has \(\Delta\phi = \pi/2\), introducing a quarter-wavelength path difference.	eo h1_waveplates
State the Jones matrix for a half-wave plate (fast axis along Ox).	\( \underline{\underline{J}}_{\lambda/2,x} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\0 & -1\end{pmatrix} \)<br><br>Has \(\Delta\phi = \pi\). Reflects polarization about the fast axis.	eo h1_waveplates
What does a quarter-wave plate do to linearly polarized light at 45° to the fast axis?	Produces circularly polarized light. The two equal components emerge with a \(\pi/2\) phase difference, and their superposition traces a circle.	eo h1_waveplates
What does a half-wave plate do to plane-polarized light?	Rotates the plane of polarization. If the incident E is at angle \(\theta\) to the fast axis, the emerging E is at \(-\theta\). For \(\theta=45°\), the polarization is rotated by 90°.	eo h1_waveplates
What is photoelasticity?	Stress-induced birefringence: an isotropic transparent material becomes birefringent when subjected to mechanical stress, because the molecular distortion changes the dielectric tensor. Used to visualise stress patterns by viewing between crossed polarisers.	eo h1_inducedbi
Distinguish the Kerr and Pockels effects.	Kerr effect: in liquids/gases, applied E-field induces uniaxial birefringence with \(\Delta n = \lambda_0 K E_0^2\) (quadratic in E because optical properties cannot depend on the field's sign in an isotropic medium).<br><br>Pockels effect: in crystals lacking inversion symmetry, the induced birefringence can be linear in E.<br><br>Both used to make voltage-controlled waveplates / fast optical modulators (ns switching).	eo h1_inducedbi
What is a chiral material?	A material whose molecular structure cannot be superposed on its mirror image (i.e. has handedness). Examples: amino acids, sugars, quartz. Optical chirality is built in even in liquid form.	eo h1_optical_activity
What does an optically active material do to LCP and RCP light?	It has different refractive indices \(n_L \neq n_R\) for the two circular polarizations. These are the natural characteristic waves of a chiral medium (analogous to how linear polarizations are characteristic of a birefringent medium).	eo h1_optical_activity
Show that an optically active medium rotates the plane of plane-polarized light.	Decompose \(\underline{L}_x = (\underline{C}_R + \underline{C}_L)/\sqrt{2}\). After traversing thickness d, each component picks up a phase \(e^{i\omega n_{R,L}d/c}\). Recombining gives plane-polarized light rotated by \(\Delta\phi/2 = \pi(n_L - n_R)d/\lambda\).	eo h1_optical_activity
Define the specific rotatory power.	\( \alpha = \dfrac{\pi(n_L - n_R)}{\lambda} = \dfrac{1}{2}(k_L - k_R) = \dfrac{\omega}{2c}(n_L - n_R) \)<br><br>Rotation per unit length. \(\alpha > 0\): dextrorotatory (clockwise from observer's view). \(\alpha < 0\): levorotatory.	eo h1_optical_activity
What is the Faraday effect?	Magnetically induced optical activity: an applied \(\mathbf{B}_0\) field along the propagation direction makes an isotropic medium chiral, rotating the plane of polarization. Different from natural chirality because \(\mathbf{B}_0\) is directional and reversing the propagation direction reverses the rotation.	eo h1_optical_activity
Set up the equation of motion for an electron in a plasma in the Faraday geometry.	With \(\mathbf{B}_0\parallel\hat{z}\) and EM wave \(\mathbf{E}\propto e^{-i\omega t}\):<br><br>\( m\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = -e(\mathbf{E} + \dot{\mathbf{r}}\times\mathbf{B}_0) \)<br><br>The B-field of the wave is neglected. Coupled equations for \(x_0\) and \(y_0\) introduce the cyclotron frequency \(\omega_c = eB_0/m\).	eo h1_optical_activity
State the dielectric responses for LCP and RCP waves in a magnetised plasma.	\( \varepsilon_{L/R}(\omega) = 1 - \dfrac{\omega_p^2}{\omega(\omega \mp \omega_c)} \)<br><br>where \(\omega_p\) is the plasma frequency and \(\omega_c = eB_0/m\) is the cyclotron frequency. The minus sign is for LCP, plus for RCP.	eo h1_optical_activity
Define the Verdet coefficient.	\( \theta = V B_0 d \)<br><br>where \(\theta\) is the Faraday rotation angle, \(B_0\) the applied magnetic field, and \(d\) the path length in the medium. Verdet coefficients vary strongly with wavelength: ~\(10^{-3}\) rad/(T·m) for gases, ~3 for water, 25–250 for dense glasses.	eo h1_optical_activity
State the Faraday rotation angle in a plasma in the small-field limit.	\( \theta \approx -\dfrac{\omega_p^2 \omega_c d}{2c\omega^2 \sqrt{1-\omega_p^2/\omega^2}} \)<br><br>Linear in \(B_0\) (through \(\omega_c\)), so this defines a Verdet coefficient.	eo h1_optical_activity
Why is the dielectric tensor of a magnetised plasma Hermitian but not real symmetric?	Off-diagonal terms \(\varepsilon_{xy} = i\omega_c\omega_p^2 / [\omega(\omega^2-\omega_c^2)]\) are purely imaginary, making the tensor Hermitian (\(\varepsilon_{ij} = \varepsilon_{ji}^*\)) but not real symmetric. This imaginary off-diagonal structure is the signature of magnetically-induced chirality.	eo h1_optical_activity
Why don't perpendicularly polarized waves interfere?	The Poynting flux from two superposed perpendicularly plane-polarized waves equals the sum of their individual fluxes — there is no cross term. Their E vectors lie along different axes so the time-averaged interference cross-term vanishes.	eo h1_partial_pol
Define the degree of polarization V.	\( V = \dfrac{I_{\mathrm{pol}}}{I_{\mathrm{pol}} + I_{\mathrm{unpol}}} \)<br><br>For a beam containing both polarized and unpolarized light. \(V=1\) for fully polarized; \(V=0\) for unpolarized.	eo h1_partial_pol
State the three Fresnel-Arago laws.	(i) Two beams with parallel polarisation planes interfere if coherent.<br>(ii) Two beams with perpendicular polarisation planes do not interfere even if coherent.<br>(iii) Two beams with parallel polarisations cannot interfere if derived from perpendicularly polarised components of unpolarised light (because those are mutually incoherent).	eo h1_partial_pol
Why do real light sources have finite coherence?	Pure monochromatic waves require infinite extent in time and space (Fourier theory). Real sources (even lasers) emit only quasi-monochromatic light, with phase registration lost over a coherence time \(\tau_c\) (or coherence length \(l_c = c\tau_c\)).	eo h1_coherence
State the relationship between the FT of a function and its power spectrum.	\( P(\omega)\,d\omega \sim |F(\omega)|^2\,d\omega \)<br><br>where \(F(\omega)\) is the Fourier transform of the time-dependent waveform. Power per unit frequency.	eo h1_coherence
State the FT convention used in the coherence section.	\( f(t) = \dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty F(\omega)e^{i\omega t}\,d\omega \)<br><br>\( F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt \)	eo h1_coherence
Describe lifetime broadening and give the resulting lineshape.	An isolated atom emitting unstimulated radiation acts as a damped harmonic oscillator with decay time \(\tau_s\). Its spectrum is Lorentzian:<br><br>\( P(\omega) \propto \dfrac{1}{(\omega-\omega_0)^2 + \omega_s^2} \)<br><br>FWHM \(= 2\omega_s = 2/\tau_s\).	eo h1_coherence
Describe Doppler (thermal) broadening.	Atoms moving with thermal velocity \(v_x\) along the line of sight produce a Doppler shift \(\omega - \omega_0 = \omega_0 v_x/c\). The Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution gives a Gaussian lineshape:<br><br>\( P(\omega) = C\exp\!\left[-\dfrac{(\omega-\omega_0)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right] \)<br><br>FWHM = \(2.36\,\omega_0\sqrt{k_B T/(mc^2)}\). Depends on temperature.	eo h1_coherence
Describe pressure broadening.	Collisions between atoms randomise the phase of the emitted wave. Mean time between collisions \(\tau_1 \propto 1/p\) (at fixed T). Produces a Lorentzian lineshape with width \(\sim 1/\tau_1\). Lamps run at low pressure for sharp lines.	eo h1_coherence
Define the mutual coherence function.	\( \Gamma(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \tau) = \langle f_1(\mathbf{r}_1, t) f_2^*(\mathbf{r}_2, t-\tau)\rangle \)<br><br>The time-averaged correlation between the wavefield at two points and at time delay \(\tau\). The complex degree of mutual coherence is \(\gamma = \Gamma/\sqrt{I_1 I_2}\), with \(|\gamma|\le 1\).	eo h1_coherence
Relate the measured intensity in an interferometer to the coherence function.	\( I(\tau) = 2I_0 + 2\,\mathrm{Re}[\Gamma(\tau)] = 2I_0 + 2|\Gamma(\tau)|\cos\Delta(\tau) \)<br><br>where \(\tau = 2d/c\) is the time delay introduced. For quasi-monochromatic light, the cosine oscillates rapidly and the envelope \(|\Gamma|\) decreases with \(\tau\).	eo h1_coherence
Define fringe visibility.	\( V = \dfrac{I_{\max} - I_{\min}}{I_{\max} + I_{\min}} = \dfrac{|\Gamma(\tau)|}{I_0} = |\gamma(\tau)| \)<br><br>Equals the modulus of the normalised coherence function. \(V=1\) for fully coherent, \(V=0\) for incoherent.	eo h1_coherence
State the Wiener-Khinchine theorem.	The Fourier transform of the autocorrelation function of a function equals the squared modulus of the FT of the function:<br><br>\( \mathrm{FT}[h(\tau)] = |F(\omega)|^2 \)<br><br>Hence the power spectrum is \(P(\omega) \sim \mathrm{FT}[\gamma(\tau)]\): the coherence function and the power spectrum are Fourier conjugates.	eo h1_coherence
How does Fourier transform spectroscopy work?	A Michelson interferometer scans the path difference \(2d\) and records the interferogram \(I(\tau)\). Fourier-transforming this (after subtracting \(2I_0\)) gives the power spectrum \(P(\omega)\) by the Wiener-Khinchine theorem. Particularly powerful in the IR (FTIR), with Jacquinot and Fellgett advantages.	eo h1_coherence
Define the coherence length and relate it to spectral linewidth.	\( l_c \) = path difference \(2d\) at which fringe visibility falls to \(1/e\). For a Gaussian line of FWHM \(\delta\omega = 2.36\sigma\):<br><br>\( l_c \approx \dfrac{\lambda^2}{2\,\delta\lambda} \)<br><br>For Kr-84 (\(\delta\lambda = 2\times 10^{-4}\) nm at 560 nm): \(l_c \approx 0.78\) m.	eo h1_coherence
What are the Jacquinot and Fellgett advantages of FTIR?	Jacquinot (throughput): no input slit needed, so the étendue and detector area can be much larger than in a grating spectrometer.<br><br>Fellgett (multiplex): all wavelengths fall on the detector simultaneously throughout the scan, improving SNR for detector-noise-limited measurements (typical in IR).	eo h1_coherence
Define the complex degree of lateral (spatial) coherence.	\( \gamma(u) = \dfrac{1}{I_0}\int I(\theta)e^{-iu\theta}\,d\theta = \dfrac{\mathrm{FT}[I(\theta)]}{I_0} \)<br><br>where \(u = kd\) (slit separation times wavenumber) and \(I(\theta)\) is the angular intensity distribution of the source.	eo h1_coherence
State the van Cittert-Zernike theorem.	The complex degree of lateral coherence is the (normalized) Fourier transform of the angular intensity distribution of an incoherent source:<br><br>\( \gamma(u) = \dfrac{\mathrm{FT}[I(\theta)]}{I_0} \)<br><br>Larger source → narrower coherence width.	eo h1_coherence
Calculate the coherence width for a uniform line source of angular width α.	\( \gamma(u) = \mathrm{sinc}(u\alpha/2) \), giving first zero at \(u\alpha/2 = \pi\), so:<br><br>\( w_c = \lambda/\alpha \)<br><br>The slits must be closer than this for fringes to be visible.	eo h1_coherence
Calculate the coherence width for a uniform disc source of angular diameter α.	\( \gamma(u) = \dfrac{2 J_1(u\alpha/2)}{u\alpha/2} \), where \(J_1\) is a Bessel function. First zero at \(u\alpha/2 = 3.83\):<br><br>\( w_c = \dfrac{1.22\lambda}{\alpha} \)<br><br>Same Airy-like form as the diffraction limit.	eo h1_coherence
How did Michelson use a stellar interferometer to measure star diameters?	Light is gathered by widely separated mirrors (variable spacing D) but interfered through closely-spaced fixed slits (separation d). This gives well-spaced fringes whose visibility falls as D increases. The D at which fringes vanish gives the angular diameter \(\alpha = 1.22\lambda/D\). For Betelgeuse (\(\lambda=570\)nm, fringes vanished at \(D=3.07\)m), \(\alpha \approx 0.047\) arcsec.	eo h1_coherence
What is phase closure in interferometric imaging?	Atmospheric and instrumental perturbations corrupt the measured phase \(\beta\) of \(\gamma\) on millisecond timescales. Why introduce the magnetic vector potential A? This couples to the electron's spin magnetic moment:<br><br>\( H_{so} = \dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{ge}{4mc^2}\dfrac{1}{r}\dfrac{d\Phi}{dr}(\mathbf{s}\cdot\mathbf{L}) \)<br><br>The factor of ½ is the Thomas precession correction.	eo h3_EBtransform
State the E-field of a uniformly moving point charge.	Lorentz-transforming the rest-frame Coulomb field:<br><br>\( E_\parallel = \dfrac{q\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\gamma^2 r^2(1 - \beta^2\sin^2\theta)^{3/2}} \)<br><br>\( E_\perp = \dfrac{q\sin\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\gamma^2 r^2(1 - \beta^2\sin^2\theta)^{3/2}} \)<br><br>E is purely radial (points from present position) but has anisotropic magnitude.	eo h3_uniformcharge
State the B-field of a uniformly moving point charge.	\( B_\phi = (v/c^2)E_\perp \)<br><br>Azimuthal magnetic field, perpendicular to both v and E. Vanishes in the rest frame.	eo h3_uniformcharge
Why does a uniformly moving charge not radiate?	Because in its rest frame it has zero acceleration and zero radiation. By Lorentz invariance of total radiated power, no radiation in any frame. Equivalently: \(N\propto 1/r^4\) (not 1/r²), so no net outward energy flux — the Poynting flux just transports field energy along with the charge.	eo h3_uniformcharge
What happens to E for an ultrarelativistic uniformly moving charge?	For \(\gamma\gg 1\):<br>• Near \(\theta = 0\) or \(\pi\): \(E_\parallel \sim q/(\gamma^2 4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)\) — very small.<br>• Near \(\theta = \pi/2\): \(E_\perp \sim \gamma q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)\) — very large.<br><br>The field is flattened into a disc perpendicular to the motion ('pancaking').	eo h3_uniformcharge
State the Čerenkov condition.	Radiation is emitted when a charged particle moves through a medium faster than light in that medium:<br><br>\( v > \dfrac{c}{n} \)<br><br>Equivalently \(\beta n > 1\), or \(\beta^2\varepsilon(\omega) > 1\) for the relevant frequency components.	eo h3_uniformcharge
Derive the Čerenkov angle.	By Huygens construction, wavelets emitted along the particle's track interfere constructively along the cone tangent to all of them. In time T, the particle moves vT while the wavelet expands to (c/n)T:<br><br>\( \cos\theta_C = \dfrac{c/n}{v} = \dfrac{1}{\beta n} \)<br><br>The cone angle from the particle direction is \(\theta_C\).	eo h3_uniformcharge
Where is Čerenkov radiation observed?	(i) Particle showers in the atmosphere from cosmic rays.<br>(ii) Blue glow surrounding water-cooled nuclear reactors (relativistic β-particles in water with n=1.33).<br>(iii) Čerenkov particle detectors (e.g. Super-Kamiokande, SNO) — tanks of water surrounded by photomultipliers.	eo h3_uniformcharge
Why does Čerenkov radiation typically appear blue?	The intensity per unit frequency is \(d^2 E/(d\omega\,dx) \propto \omega(1 - 1/\beta^2\varepsilon(\omega))\), which is roughly linear in \(\omega\). So the higher-frequency (blue) end dominates over the red end of the visible spectrum — blue glow.	eo h3_uniformcharge
Describe qualitatively why an accelerated charge radiates.	When a charge changes velocity, the field lines develop a transverse 'kink' propagating outward at speed c. For \(r>ct\), the field still emanates from the charge's would-be (uniform-motion) position; for \(r<ct\), from its actual position. The kink connecting these is the radiative field.	eo h3_accelerated
What sets the characteristic wavelength of radiation from an accelerating charge?	\( \lambda \sim c|u|/|a| \)<br><br>where |u| is a characteristic speed and |a| a characteristic acceleration. For circular motion this gives \(\lambda \sim c/\omega_B = R/\beta\).	eo h3_accelerated
State the Liénard-Wiechert scalar and vector potentials.	\( \phi(\mathbf{x},t) = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\dfrac{e}{(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{n})R}\right]_{\mathrm{ret}} \)<br><br>\( \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x},t) = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\left[\dfrac{e\mathbf{v}}{(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{n})R}\right]_{\mathrm{ret}} \)<br><br>where R is the retarded distance and n is the unit vector from the (retarded) charge to the field point. The denominator factor accounts for the Doppler-like compression of the source's effective time.	eo h3_accelerated
State the Liénard-Wiechert E-field of a point charge.	\( \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x},t) = \dfrac{e}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left\{\left[\dfrac{\mathbf{n}-\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\gamma^2(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{n})^3 R^2}\right]_{\mathrm{ret}} + \left[\dfrac{\mathbf{n}\times((\mathbf{n}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\times\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{c(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{n})^3 R}\right]_{\mathrm{ret}}\right\} \)<br><br>First term: velocity field (\(\propto 1/R^2\), reproduces Coulomb-like static field for uniform motion). Second term: acceleration / radiation field (\(\propto 1/R\)).	eo h3_accelerated
State the Liénard-Wiechert B-field of a point charge.	\( \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t) = \dfrac{1}{c}[\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{E}]_{\mathrm{ret}} \)<br><br>Always perpendicular to E and to n (the retarded line of sight to the charge).	eo h3_accelerated
Distinguish velocity fields and acceleration fields in the Liénard-Wiechert formula.	Velocity fields: independent of \(\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\), fall as \(1/R^2\), essentially static fields carried with the charge (reproduce §8.1 result for uniform motion).<br><br>Acceleration fields: linear in \(\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\), fall as \(1/R\), give Poynting flux \(\propto 1/R^2\) — the true radiation fields.	eo h3_accelerated
State the (non-relativistic) Larmor formula.	\( P = \dfrac{\mu_0 e^2 |\dot{\mathbf{v}}|^2}{6\pi c} \)<br><br>Total instantaneous power radiated by a non-relativistic accelerated charge. Same as a Hertzian dipole with \(\mathbf{p} = e\mathbf{r}\) and \(\ddot{\mathbf{p}} = e\dot{\mathbf{v}}\).	eo h3_larmor
State the angular distribution of Larmor radiation.	\( \dfrac{dP}{d\Omega} = \dfrac{\mu_0 e^2}{16\pi^2 c}|\dot{\mathbf{v}}|^2\sin^2\theta \)<br><br>where θ is the angle between the acceleration \(\dot{\mathbf{v}}\) and the observation direction \(\mathbf{n}\). Same \(\sin^2\theta\) pattern as the Hertzian dipole.	eo h3_larmor
Why is total radiated power Lorentz invariant?	In the rest frame S', a small radiated 4-momentum is \(dP'^\alpha = (dE'/c, 0)\) (the radiation has the symmetric \(\sin^2\theta\) pattern, carrying no net 3-momentum). Lorentz transforming gives \(dE = \gamma dE'\). Since \(dt = \gamma dt'\), we have \(P = dE/dt = dE'/dt' = P'\). Power is invariant.	eo h3_larmor
State the Liénard formula (relativistic Larmor).	\( P = \dfrac{\mu_0 e^2}{6\pi c}(\gamma^6 a_\parallel^2 + \gamma^4 a_\perp^2) \)<br><br>where \(a_\parallel\) and \(a_\perp\) are the accelerations parallel and perpendicular to the velocity in the lab frame. Recovers Larmor for \(\gamma\to 1\).	eo h3_larmor
Derive the Liénard formula via Lorentz invariance.	The Larmor invariant is \(\alpha^2 = -A_{cc}\cdot A_{cc}\), where \(A_{cc}^\alpha = (\gamma_u^4 \dot u u/c, \gamma_u^4(\dot u u/c^2)\mathbf{u} + \gamma_u^2 \mathbf{a})\) is the 4-acceleration. Evaluating \(-A_{cc}\cdot A_{cc} = \gamma_u^6(\dot u u)^2/c^2 + \gamma_u^4 a^2\), and decomposing \(a^2 = a_\parallel^2 + a_\perp^2\) (with \(\dot u = a_\parallel\)), gives \(\alpha^2 = \gamma^6 a_\parallel^2 + \gamma^4 a_\perp^2\). Then \(P = \mu_0 e^2 \alpha^2/(6\pi c)\).	eo h3_larmor
For linear acceleration vs circular motion, which dominates radiation losses?	For comparable accelerations, circular motion (with \(a_\perp\) only, factor \(\gamma^4\)) loses energy more easily than linear acceleration (with \(a_\parallel\) only, factor \(\gamma^6\)) ONLY if the linear acceleration is artificially small. In particle accelerators the relevant comparison is at fixed force/applied field: linear accelerators are preferred at very high energies because circular synchrotrons suffer huge \(\gamma^4\) radiative losses on tight orbits.	eo h3_larmor
State the equation of motion for a relativistic charge in a uniform B-field.	\( \dfrac{d(\gamma m\mathbf{v})}{dt} = e(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}) \)<br><br>Since \(\mathbf{f}\perp\mathbf{v}\), the kinetic energy and γ are constant (assuming radiative losses small). For \(\mathbf{B}=B\hat{z}\), \(v_z=0\): circular motion in the xy-plane.	eo h3_circular
Define the gyrofrequency.	\( \omega_B = \dfrac{eB}{\gamma m} \)<br><br>Angular frequency of relativistic circular motion in field B. Reduces to non-relativistic cyclotron frequency \(eB/m\) for \(\gamma\to 1\). Orbital radius \(R = u/\omega_B = \gamma m u/(eB)\).	eo h3_circular
State the radial acceleration for circular motion in a B-field.	\( a_\perp = \dfrac{evB}{\gamma m} = v\omega_B \)<br><br>Substituting into the Liénard formula gives \( P = \mu_0 e^4 \gamma^2 B^2 v^2 / (6\pi c m^2) \) — the instantaneous radiative power loss for a relativistic charge in a magnetic field.	eo h3_circular
Why is the dipole approximation valid for non-relativistic cyclotron radiation?	For \(v\ll c\), \(\gamma\to 1\). The wavelength \(\lambda = 2\pi c/\omega_B\) and the orbital radius \(R = v/\omega_B\) satisfy \(\lambda/R = 2\pi c/v \gg 1\). So the source is small compared to the wavelength — dipole approximation applies.	eo h3_cyclotron
Describe non-relativistic cyclotron radiation as a sum of Hertzian dipoles.	Circular motion ≡ two perpendicular Hertzian dipoles oscillating in quadrature: \(\mathbf{p}(t) = \hat{x}p_0\cos\omega_B t + \hat{y}p_0\sin\omega_B t\) with \(p_0 = eR\). Their (time-averaged) Poynting fluxes add since they're π/2 out of phase.	eo h3_cyclotron
State the time-averaged power for non-relativistic cyclotron radiation.	\( \langle P\rangle = \dfrac{\omega_B^2 e^2 v^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3} \)<br><br>Monochromatic at the cyclotron frequency \(\omega_B = eB/m\). The factor of 2 arises from two perpendicular dipoles contributing equally.	eo h3_cyclotron
Describe the polarization of cyclotron radiation as a function of viewing direction.	(i) In the equatorial plane (the orbital plane): only one of the two perpendicular dipoles is effective → linearly polarized in that plane.<br>(ii) Along the polar axis (perpendicular to orbit): both dipoles contribute equally with π/2 phase → circularly polarized.<br>(iii) At intermediate angles → elliptically polarized.	eo h3_cyclotron
Why does the dipole-approximation cyclotron picture break down for relativistic motion?	For \(\gamma\gg 1\), the characteristic emitted wavelength becomes \(\lambda_s \sim R/\gamma^3 \ll R\), so the source is much larger than the wavelength. The dipole approximation fails; radiation is not harmonic but pulsed in time, with a broad spectrum centred on \(\nu_s = c/\lambda_s\).	eo h3_synchrotron
State the angular distribution of radiation from a relativistic accelerating charge.	\( \dfrac{dP(t')}{d\Omega} = \dfrac{e^2\mu_0 c}{16\pi^2}\dfrac{|\mathbf{n}\times((\mathbf{n}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\times\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}})|^2}{(1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{n})^5} \)<br><br>The factor \((1-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{n})^{-5}\) produces strong forward beaming for ultrarelativistic motion.	eo h3_synchrotron
Describe relativistic beaming.	For \(\gamma\gg 1\), the radiation is concentrated into a narrow forward cone of half-angle \(\sim 1/\gamma\) around the velocity vector. As β increases the rear-lobe vanishes and the forward lobe sharpens — the 'searchlight effect'.	eo h3_synchrotron
Estimate the synchrotron pulse width seen by a stationary observer.	The observer sees radiation only over a particle arc of length \(2R/\gamma\) (the part where the forward cone of half-angle \(1/\gamma\) points at them). Light propagation effects compress this further:<br><br>\( \Delta t \approx \dfrac{2R}{\gamma}\left(\dfrac{1}{v} - \dfrac{1}{c}\right) = \dfrac{2}{\gamma\omega_B}(1-v/c) \sim \dfrac{1}{\gamma^3 \omega_B} \)<br><br>using \(1-v/c \approx 1/(2\gamma^2)\).	eo h3_synchrotron
State the characteristic synchrotron frequency.	\( \nu_s \sim \dfrac{1}{\Delta t} \sim \gamma^3 \omega_B \)<br><br>So \(\lambda_s \sim R/\gamma^3\). The spectrum is broad (pulsed emission) with critical frequency \(\nu_c = (3/4\pi)\nu_s\). Vastly higher than the gyrofrequency for \(\gamma\gg 1\).	eo h3_synchrotron
State the polarization properties of synchrotron radiation.	Highly polarized:<br>• In the orbital plane: linearly polarized parallel to the plane.<br>• Slightly off-plane: elliptically polarized.<br>• Net polarization survives integration because of the strong beaming.<br><br>Useful astrophysical diagnostic — distinguishes synchrotron emission from thermal sources.	eo h3_synchrotron
Where does synchrotron radiation occur?	Astrophysical: supernova remnants (Crab nebula), radio galaxies (Cygnus A — jets and lobes), pulsars, AGN.<br>Laboratory: storage rings (ESRF, Diamond) used as bright collimated X-ray sources for spectroscopy and crystallography.<br>It is the dominant energy-loss mechanism in circular electron accelerators, limiting their maximum energy.	eo h3_synchrotron
Why are linear accelerators preferred for high-energy electrons?	Synchrotron losses scale as \(\gamma^4\) at fixed orbit radius. For a given energy, radiative losses become catastrophic in circular machines for very high \(\gamma\) — the energy supplied by the accelerating cavities is lost to synchrotron radiation. Linear accelerators avoid the centripetal acceleration entirely.	eo h3_synchrotron
State the typical scaling of energy loss per orbit in a synchrotron.	\( \Delta E_{\mathrm{orbit}} \propto \dfrac{\gamma^4}{R} \)<br><br>Strong incentive for large rings (e.g. LEP at CERN had circumference 27 km). At the ESRF (γ ≈ 12000, R ≈ 134 m), characteristic wavelength \(\lambda_s \sim 5\) Å — hard X-rays.	eo h3_synchrotron
Summarise the four field zones around a Hertzian dipole.	Going outward: (1) Static near zone: dipole term dominates, \(E\propto 1/r^3\), \(B\propto 1/r^2\). (2) Induction (intermediate) zone: \(r\sim\lambda\), all terms comparable. (3) Radiation (far) zone: \(r\gg\lambda\), only \(E\propto 1/r\) and \(B\propto 1/r\) survive, giving \(N\propto 1/r^2\) — true outgoing radiation.	eo summary
List the four key wave-related Lorentz invariants in EM.	(1) Electric charge q. (2) Phase \(k_\alpha x^\alpha = \omega t - \mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}\) of a plane wave (so \(k^\alpha = (\omega/c, \mathbf{k})\) is a 4-vector). (3) The d'Alembertian \(\Box^2\). (4) Total radiated power \(P = dE/dt\).	eo summary
Compare scaling of total power radiated with frequency for ED, MD, and EQ radiation.	Electric dipole: \(\langle P\rangle \propto \omega^4\)<br>Magnetic dipole: \(\langle P\rangle \propto \omega^4\), but suppressed by \((a/\lambda)^2\) compared to ED of similar size.<br>Electric quadrupole: \(\langle P\rangle \propto \omega^6\), suppressed similarly.<br><br>Higher multipoles increasingly suppressed for \(ka\ll 1\).	eo summary
Compare scaling of cross-section with frequency for Rayleigh and Thomson scattering.	Rayleigh (bound electrons, \(\omega\ll\omega_0\)): \(\sigma \propto \omega^4 \propto 1/\lambda^4\). Sky is blue.<br><br>Thomson (free electrons): \(\sigma_T = (8\pi/3)r_e^2\), independent of frequency. Important in plasmas.<br><br>Transition: when driving frequency comparable to atomic resonances, the polarizability \(\alpha(\omega)\) becomes resonant — strong absorption/scattering.	eo summary
Summarise the Wiener-Khinchine and van Cittert-Zernike theorems.	Wiener-Khinchine (temporal coherence): \(P(\omega) \propto \mathrm{FT}[\gamma(\tau)]\) — the power spectrum is the FT of the temporal autocorrelation. Underlies FTIR.<br><br>Van Cittert-Zernike (spatial coherence): \(\gamma(u) = \mathrm{FT}[I(\theta)]/I_0\) — the lateral coherence function is the FT of the angular intensity distribution of the source. Underlies aperture synthesis imaging.	eo summary
Compare the structures of Maxwell's equations and the wave equations for A and ϕ in different gauges.	Coulomb gauge (\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}=0\)): \(\nabla^2\phi = -\rho/\varepsilon_0\) (instantaneous Poisson — non-causal but unphysical info doesn't propagate); \(\nabla^2\mathbf{A} - c^{-2}\ddot{\mathbf{A}} = -\mu_0\mathbf{J}_T\) (transverse current source).<br><br>Lorenz gauge (\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}+c^{-2}\dot\phi=0\)): \(\Box^2\phi = \rho/\varepsilon_0\) and \(\Box^2\mathbf{A} = \mu_0\mathbf{J}\) — symmetric, manifestly Lorentz covariant.	eo summary
In one sentence, what is the conceptual significance of the Aharonov-Bohm effect?	Quantum mechanics shows that the vector potential A is a real physical field — not just a mathematical convenience — because charged particles can detect A through interference even in regions where B = 0.	eo summary
In one sentence, what is the conceptual significance of magnetism as a relativistic effect?	Magnetic forces are simply the relativistic transformation of electrostatic forces between moving charges, with the magnetic and electric fields being two components of a single antisymmetric field-strength tensor \(F^{\alpha\beta}\).	eo summary
In one sentence, what is the conceptual significance of Čerenkov radiation?	It is the electromagnetic analogue of a sonic boom — coherent radiation emitted on a Mach-like cone whenever a charged particle exceeds the local phase velocity of light in a medium.	eo summary
