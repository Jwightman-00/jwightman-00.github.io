#--- POSTULATES OF QUANTUM MECHANICS ---
What are the four postulates of quantum mechanics?	1. State: physical state described by normalised wavefunction ψ in a Hilbert space. 2. Observables: represented by Hermitian operators; eigenvalues are possible measurement outcomes. 3. Born rule: P(aᵢ) = |⟨ψᵢ|ψ⟩|² = |cᵢ|²; on measurement, wavefunction collapses to eigenstate ψᵢ. 4. Dynamics: state evolves by iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ.	postulates
What is the Born rule?	The probability of measuring eigenvalue aᵢ of observable  is P(aᵢ) = |⟨ψᵢ|ψ⟩|² = |cᵢ|², where |ψ⟩ = Σ cᵢ|ψᵢ⟩ expands the state in eigenstates. Expectation value: ⟨A⟩ = Σ aᵢ|cᵢ|².	postulates
What happens to the wavefunction upon measurement?	It collapses to the eigenstate |ψᵢ⟩ corresponding to the measured eigenvalue aᵢ. If aᵢ is degenerate, the state projects onto the degenerate subspace. Measurement irreversibly alters the state.	postulates
Write the Time-Dependent Schrödinger Equation (TDSE). What is its solution for an energy eigenstate?	iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ. For an energy eigenstate with Ĥψₙ = Eₙψₙ: ψ(r,t) = ψ(r,0) exp(−iEₙt/ℏ). The time dependence is a global phase — stationary states have time-independent probability densities.	postulates
Why must observables be represented by Hermitian operators?	(1) Eigenvalues of Hermitian operators are real → measurement outcomes are real numbers. (2) Eigenstates of a Hermitian operator form a complete orthonormal basis → any state can be expanded as |ψ⟩ = Σcᵢ|ψᵢ⟩, enabling the Born rule. (3) Hermitian operators generate unitary time evolution.	postulates

#--- MATHEMATICAL FRAMEWORK ---
What is the Heisenberg picture and how does an operator evolve in it?	In the Heisenberg picture, operators carry all time dependence and states are fixed. The equation of motion for operator B̂ is: dB̂/dt = (i/ℏ)[Ĥ, B̂(t)] + ∂B̂/∂t. Contrast with the Schrödinger picture (states evolve, operators are fixed). Expectation values are the same in both pictures.	formalism
State the canonical commutation relations.	[x̂ᵢ, p̂ⱼ] = iℏ δᵢⱼ, [x̂ᵢ, x̂ⱼ] = 0, [p̂ᵢ, p̂ⱼ] = 0. In position representation: p̂ = −iℏ∇, so p̂ψ(x) = −iℏ dψ/dx. These commutation relations encode wave-particle duality and are the foundation of quantum mechanics.	formalism
State the generalised uncertainty principle.	For any two observables  and B̂: ΔA · ΔB ≥ ½|⟨[Â, B̂]⟩|, where ΔA = √(⟨Â²⟩ − ⟨Â⟩²). For position and momentum: Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ/2. The uncertainty is fundamental — it is NOT due to measurement disturbance but reflects the non-commutativity of operators.	formalism
What is the Ehrenfest theorem?	Expectation values obey classical equations of motion: d⟨x⟩/dt = ⟨p⟩/m and d⟨p⟩/dt = −⟨∂V/∂x⟩. In general: d⟨B̂⟩/dt = (i/ℏ)⟨[Ĥ, B̂]⟩ + ⟨∂B̂/∂t⟩. This shows classical mechanics emerges from QM in the limit where quantum fluctuations are negligible.	formalism
What is a Complete Set of Commuting Observables (CSCO)?	A maximal set of mutually commuting Hermitian operators whose simultaneous eigenstates form a complete basis for the Hilbert space. Two operators share eigenstates if and only if they commute. The quantum numbers from a CSCO uniquely label every state. Example: {Ĥ, L̂², L̂z} for the hydrogen atom.	formalism

#--- HARMONIC OSCILLATOR ---
Define the ladder operators â and â† for the harmonic oscillator. What is the Hamiltonian in terms of them?	â = (mωx̂ + ip̂)/√(2mωℏ), â† = (mωx̂ − ip̂)/√(2mωℏ). Commutator: [â, â†] = 1. Number operator: n̂ = â†â. Hamiltonian: Ĥ = ℏω(n̂ + 1/2) = ℏω(â†â + 1/2). The energy eigenvalues are Eₙ = ℏω(n + 1/2), n = 0, 1, 2, ...	harmonic-oscillator
What are the energy levels of the quantum harmonic oscillator and what is the zero-point energy?	Eₙ = ℏω(n + 1/2), for n = 0, 1, 2, ... Zero-point energy E₀ = ℏω/2 > 0. The ground state has non-zero energy — a direct consequence of the uncertainty principle: confining the particle in x necessarily gives nonzero ⟨p²⟩.	harmonic-oscillator
How do â and â† act on number (Fock) states |n⟩?	â|n⟩ = √n |n−1⟩ (lowering / annihilation). â†|n⟩ = √(n+1) |n+1⟩ (raising / creation). n̂|n⟩ = n|n⟩. Ground state: â|0⟩ = 0. General state: |n⟩ = (â†)ⁿ/√(n!) |0⟩. Position matrix elements: ⟨n|x̂|m⟩ ∝ δ_{n,m±1} (only adjacent transitions).	harmonic-oscillator
Why is the harmonic oscillator so central to physics?	Any potential with a stable equilibrium can be Taylor-expanded as V ≈ V₀ + ½V''x² near the minimum — a harmonic potential. The ladder operator formalism applies universally: phonons (lattice vibrations), photons (EM field modes), and all quantum fields are collections of harmonic oscillators. QFT is built entirely on this structure.	harmonic-oscillator
What is Compton scattering and what does it demonstrate?	X-ray photon scatters off a free electron; the wavelength shift is Δλ = λ_C(1 − cosθ), where the Compton wavelength λ_C = h/(mₑc) ≈ 0.00243 nm. Demonstrates: (1) particle nature of photons (momentum p = h/λ), (2) relativistic kinematics of photon-electron collision. The classical prediction Δλ = 0 is ruled out.	historical

#--- HYDROGEN ATOM AND 3D QM ---
What are the energy levels of the hydrogen atom? What is their degeneracy?	Eₙ = −13.6 eV/n² = −mₑe⁴/(2ℏ²n²) = −α²mₑc²/(2n²), for n = 1, 2, 3, ... Each level has degeneracy 2n² (accounting for spin). States labelled by (n, l, m, mₛ): l = 0,...,n−1; m = −l,...,l; mₛ = ±½.	hydrogen-atom
Write the hydrogen atom wavefunction. What is the Bohr radius?	ψₙₗₘ(r,θ,φ) = Rₙₗ(r) Yₗᵐ(θ,φ), where Rₙₗ involves associated Laguerre polynomials and Yₗᵐ are spherical harmonics. Bohr radius: a₀ = 4πε₀ℏ²/(mₑe²) ≈ 0.529 Å. The most probable radius for the 1s state is a₀.	hydrogen-atom
What eigenvalue equations do spherical harmonics satisfy?	L̂²Yₗᵐ(θ,φ) = ℏ²l(l+1)Yₗᵐ and L̂zYₗᵐ = mℏYₗᵐ, with l = 0,1,2,... and m = −l,...,l. They are orthonormal: ∫Yₗᵐ*(θ,φ)Yₗ'ᵐ'(θ,φ)dΩ = δₗₗ'δₘₘ'. Under parity: Yₗᵐ(π−θ,φ+π) = (−1)ˡYₗᵐ(θ,φ).	angular-momentum
State the angular momentum commutation relations and their consequences.	[L̂x, L̂y] = iℏL̂z (cyclic permutations). [L̂², L̂ᵢ] = 0. Ladder operators L̂± = L̂x ± iL̂y satisfy [L̂z, L̂±] = ±ℏL̂± and L̂±|l,m⟩ = ℏ√(l(l+1)−m(m±1)) |l,m±1⟩. The algebra forces quantisation: l integer or half-integer, |m| ≤ l.	angular-momentum
What is the effective potential in the radial Schrödinger equation for hydrogen and what does it represent?	Veff(r) = V(r) + ℏ²l(l+1)/(2mr²). The centrifugal barrier term ℏ²l(l+1)/(2mr²) prevents states with l > 0 from reaching r = 0. It leads to the condition Rₙₗ ∝ rˡ as r → 0. Combined with the attractive Coulomb, it gives bound states with the correct quantum numbers.	hydrogen-atom

#--- TIME-INDEPENDENT PERTURBATION THEORY ---
State the first-order energy correction in non-degenerate perturbation theory.	For Ĥ = Ĥ₀ + Ĥ', the first-order correction to the n-th energy level is: Eₙ⁽¹⁾ = ⟨n⁰|Ĥ'|n⁰⟩, the expectation value of the perturbation in the unperturbed eigenstate. No other states contribute at first order in energy (though they do in wavefunction corrections).	perturbation-theory
State the second-order energy correction in non-degenerate perturbation theory.	Eₙ⁽²⁾ = Σ_{m≠n} |⟨m⁰|Ĥ'|n⁰⟩|² / (Eₙ⁰ − Eₘ⁰). The sum is over all unperturbed states m ≠ n. Key properties: (1) requires non-degeneracy (Eₙ ≠ Eₘ), (2) the ground state always has Eₙ⁽²⁾ < 0 (all contributions lower the energy), (3) perturbation mixes in states via matrix elements ⟨m|Ĥ'|n⟩.	perturbation-theory
What is the first-order correction to the wavefunction in perturbation theory?	|n⁽¹⁾⟩ = Σ_{m≠n} [⟨m⁰|Ĥ'|n⁰⟩ / (Eₙ⁰ − Eₘ⁰)] |m⁰⟩. The unperturbed state is not admixed (by convention ⟨n⁰|n⁽¹⁾⟩ = 0). Near-degenerate states (small energy denominator) are most strongly mixed in by the perturbation.	perturbation-theory
When and how does degenerate perturbation theory apply?	When two or more unperturbed states share the same energy Eₙ⁰, the standard non-degenerate formulae diverge. Procedure: (1) identify the degenerate subspace, (2) diagonalise the matrix [Ĥ']ᵢⱼ = ⟨nᵢ|Ĥ'|nⱼ⟩ within this subspace, (3) the eigenvalues are the first-order energy splittings and the eigenvectors are the correct zeroth-order states.	perturbation-theory
What is a "good quantum number" in the context of perturbation theory?	A quantum number associated with an operator Ô such that [Ĥ₀, Ô] = 0 AND [Ĥ', Ô] = 0. Good quantum numbers block-diagonalise the perturbation matrix, simplifying or eliminating the need to diagonalise large matrices in degenerate PT. Example: m is a good quantum number if Ĥ' is rotationally symmetric about z.	perturbation-theory
State the variational principle for estimating ground state energies.	For any normalised trial wavefunction |ψ_trial⟩: ⟨ψ_trial|Ĥ|ψ_trial⟩ ≥ E_gs, where E_gs is the exact ground state energy. Minimising ⟨Ĥ⟩ over a family of trial states (parameterised by variational parameters) gives an upper bound on E_gs. The better the trial function captures the true ground state, the tighter the bound.	perturbation-theory
Outline the WKB approximation and state the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantisation condition.	WKB applies when the potential varies slowly compared to the de Broglie wavelength. In classically allowed regions: ψ ∝ exp(±i∫p(x)dx/ℏ)/√p(x), where p(x) = √(2m(E−V(x))). Quantisation condition (including turning-point corrections): ∫_{x₁}^{x₂} p(x) dx = (n + ½)πℏ, n = 0, 1, 2, ... This gives Eₙ = ℏω(n+½) exactly for the harmonic oscillator.	perturbation-theory
How does degenerate perturbation theory explain the opening of band gaps in a weak periodic potential?	For V(x) = 2V cos(2πx/a), matrix elements are ⟨k|V|k'⟩ = V·δ_{k'−k, ±2π/a}. At the Brillouin zone boundary k = ±π/a, the free-particle states are degenerate (same energy). Diagonalising the 2×2 degenerate subspace gives two levels split by ±|V|, centred on the free-electron energy. A band gap of width 2|V| opens at each zone boundary.	perturbation-theory

#--- GAUGE INVARIANCE, AHARONOV-BOHM, LANDAU LEVELS ---
Write the Hamiltonian for a charged particle in electromagnetic fields. What gauge transformation leaves physics invariant?	Ĥ = (p̂ − qA)²/(2m) + qφ, where A is the vector potential and φ the scalar potential. Gauge transformation: A → A + ∇χ, φ → φ − ∂χ/∂t, ψ → ψ · exp(iqχ/ℏ). Physical observables (probability density, currents) are gauge-invariant. The canonical momentum p̂ = −iℏ∇ is NOT gauge-invariant; the kinetic momentum π̂ = p̂ − qA IS.	gauge-invariance
Describe the Aharonov-Bohm effect.	A charged particle travels around a solenoid (where B = 0 outside but flux Φ ≠ 0 inside). The two paths acquire different phases: Δφ = (q/ℏ)∮A·dl = qΦ/ℏ. This causes an observable interference pattern that shifts with Φ even though B = 0 along the path. Demonstrates that A (not just B) is the physically fundamental field in quantum mechanics.	gauge-invariance
What are Landau levels? Give the energy spectrum and degeneracy.	A 2D charged particle in uniform magnetic field B (along z) has quantised energies: Eₙ = ℏωc(n + ½), n = 0, 1, 2, ..., where ωc = eB/m is the cyclotron frequency. Each Landau level is highly degenerate: degeneracy per unit area = eB/h. The quantum Hall effect arises when a Landau level is partially filled.	gauge-invariance
What is the magnetic moment of an electron, and how does it couple to an external field?	Orbital magnetic moment: μ_L = −(e/2mₑ)L = −μ_B L/ℏ, where μ_B = eℏ/(2mₑ) ≈ 9.27×10⁻²⁴ J/T is the Bohr magneton. Spin magnetic moment: μ_S = −g_s μ_B S/ℏ with g_s ≈ 2. Interaction Hamiltonian: Ĥ_B = −μ·B = μ_B B(L̂z + 2Ŝz)/ℏ (z-axis along B). This gives rise to the Zeeman effect.	gauge-invariance
What does the Stern-Gerlach experiment demonstrate?	Silver atoms (with one unpaired electron, l=0, s=1/2) pass through an inhomogeneous magnetic field and split into exactly two beams (mₛ = ±½). This demonstrates: (1) spatial quantisation — angular momentum components are quantised, (2) the existence of spin-½, which has no classical analogue, (3) the g-factor: the splitting is twice what orbital angular momentum would give.	gauge-invariance

#--- SPIN AND ADDITION OF ANGULAR MOMENTUM ---
What is the spin-½ Hilbert space? Write the Pauli matrices.	Spin-½ has basis {|↑⟩, |↓⟩} (ms = +½, −½). Operators Ŝᵢ = (ℏ/2)σᵢ where the Pauli matrices are: σx = [[0,1],[1,0]], σy = [[0,−i],[i,0]], σz = [[1,0],[0,−1]]. Properties: σᵢ² = I, {σᵢ,σⱼ} = 2δᵢⱼI, [σᵢ,σⱼ] = 2iεᵢⱼₖσₖ.	spin
How do you add two angular momenta j₁ and j₂? What are the possible total values?	Total angular momentum J = j₁ ⊕ j₂ can take values J = |j₁−j₂|, |j₁−j₂|+1, ..., j₁+j₂. Conservation of mJ: M = m₁+m₂. Coupled basis |J,M⟩ = Σ ⟨j₁m₁; j₂m₂|J,M⟩ |j₁,m₁⟩|j₂,m₂⟩, where the coefficients are Clebsch-Gordan (CG) coefficients. Dimensionality check: Σ_J (2J+1) = (2j₁+1)(2j₂+1).	angular-momentum
What are Clebsch-Gordan coefficients and how are they calculated?	CG coefficients ⟨j₁m₁; j₂m₂|J,M⟩ are the matrix elements of the unitary transformation between the product basis and the coupled basis. They are real numbers satisfying M = m₁+m₂. Starting from the maximum state |J=j₁+j₂, M=j₁+j₂⟩ = |j₁,j₁⟩|j₂,j₂⟩, apply J̃₋ = J̃₁₋ + J̃₂₋ repeatedly and use orthogonality to build up all states.	angular-momentum
Write the singlet and triplet states for two spin-½ particles.	Triplet (S=1, symmetric): |1,+1⟩=|↑↑⟩, |1,0⟩=(|↑↓⟩+|↓↑⟩)/√2, |1,−1⟩=|↓↓⟩. Singlet (S=0, antisymmetric): |0,0⟩=(|↑↓⟩−|↓↑⟩)/√2. The singlet is rotationally invariant (it is a scalar). The triplet forms a 3D irrep of SU(2). For identical fermions, singlet spin pairs with symmetric spatial wavefunction (and vice versa).	spin
What is the total angular momentum J for an electron with orbital quantum number l and spin s=½?	J = l ± ½ (for l ≥ 1). For l = 0: only J = ½. Spectroscopic notation: |n, l, J, mJ⟩. The J = l+½ and J = l−½ states are split by spin-orbit coupling. The fine structure multiplets are labelled ²S+1L_J (e.g., ²P_{3/2}, ²P_{1/2} for l=1, s=½).	spin

#--- SYMMETRIES AND WIGNER-ECKART THEOREM ---
State the Wigner-Eckart theorem.	Matrix elements of a rank-k spherical tensor T^(k)_q between angular momentum eigenstates factorise as: ⟨α'j'm'|T^(k)_q|αjm⟩ = ⟨j'm'|jm;kq⟩ · ⟨α'j'||T^(k)||αj⟩. The CG coefficient ⟨j'm'|jm;kq⟩ encodes all angular momentum (geometric) dependence. The reduced matrix element ⟨α'j'||T^(k)||αj⟩ is independent of m, m', q and contains the physics.	symmetries
What selection rules follow from the Wigner-Eckart theorem for a rank-1 (vector) operator?	For a vector operator (rank-1 tensor): m' − m = q ∈ {−1, 0, +1} → Δm = 0, ±1. Angular momentum triangle rule: |j−j'| ≤ 1 ≤ j+j' → Δj = 0, ±1, but j' = 0 ↔ j = 0 is forbidden. For electric dipole transitions (r is a vector): Δl = ±1 (parity change required), Δm = 0, ±1.	symmetries
State the Landé projection formula and give its main application.	For a vector operator V̂, within a fixed-j subspace: ⟨αjm|V̂|αjm'⟩ = (⟨α j||V̂·Ĵ||α j⟩/ℏ²j(j+1)) ⟨jm|Ĵ|jm'⟩. Application: the total magnetic moment μ_J = −g_J μ_B J/ℏ within a state of definite J, where the Landé g-factor g_J = 1 + [J(J+1)+S(S+1)−L(L+1)] / [2J(J+1)] determines the Zeeman splitting.	symmetries
How does parity act on quantum states? State the parity selection rule for electric dipole transitions.	Parity P̂: ψ(r) → ψ(−r). Eigenvalues ±1 (even/odd parity). Spherical harmonics: P̂Yₗᵐ = (−1)ˡYₗᵐ. For electric dipole operator D̂ = er (which is odd under parity), non-zero matrix element ⟨f|D̂|i⟩ requires the initial and final states to have OPPOSITE parity → Δl = ±1. This is the Laporte rule.	symmetries
State Noether's theorem in quantum mechanics: how do symmetries give conservation laws?	If [Ĥ, Ô] = 0 (Ô commutes with the Hamiltonian), then d⟨Ô⟩/dt = (i/ℏ)⟨[Ĥ,Ô]⟩ = 0, so ⟨Ô⟩ is conserved. Examples: rotational symmetry [Ĥ, L̂] = 0 → angular momentum conservation; translational symmetry [Ĥ, p̂] = 0 → momentum conservation; time-translation symmetry → energy conservation. Symmetry generators are the conserved observables.	symmetries

#--- IDENTICAL PARTICLES ---
State the symmetrisation postulate for identical particles. What is its consequence?	For identical bosons: total wavefunction must be symmetric under interchange of any two particles. For identical fermions: total wavefunction must be antisymmetric. Consequence for fermions: Pauli exclusion principle — no two fermions can occupy the same quantum state (otherwise antisymmetrisation gives zero). This structures all atomic and nuclear physics.	identical-particles
What is the exchange interaction and how does it affect helium?	Antisymmetry of fermionic wavefunctions produces a correlation: electrons with parallel spins (orthohelium, triplet, S=1) must have antisymmetric spatial wavefunction → they tend to avoid each other → lower Coulomb repulsion → lower energy than parahelium (S=0). This energy splitting is the exchange interaction, which is purely quantum mechanical with no classical analogue.	identical-particles
What is the Hartree-Fock method?	A variational method for N-electron systems. Trial wavefunction: a Slater determinant of single-particle orbitals ψᵢ(r). Minimising ⟨Ĥ⟩ gives the HF equations: each orbital satisfies a one-particle Schrödinger equation with an effective potential (Hartree mean-field + exchange term). Solved self-consistently. HF neglects electron correlation (correlations beyond the mean field).	identical-particles
What is a Slater determinant?	An antisymmetrised product state for N fermions: Ψ(r₁,...,rN) = (1/√N!) det[ψᵢ(rⱼ)]. The determinant structure automatically enforces antisymmetry (swapping any two rows changes the sign) and the Pauli exclusion principle (two identical rows → determinant vanishes). It is the natural trial wavefunction in Hartree-Fock theory.	identical-particles

#--- MULTI-ELECTRON ATOMS AND ATOMIC STRUCTURE ---
Describe the central field approximation and why l-degeneracy is broken in many-electron atoms.	Replace electron-electron repulsion with an effective spherically symmetric potential U(r). Each electron moves independently: Ĥ ≈ Σᵢ [p̂ᵢ²/(2m) + U(rᵢ)]. The l-degeneracy of hydrogen is broken: electrons with smaller l penetrate closer to the nucleus (less screened by inner electrons) and are more tightly bound. Thus 2s lies below 2p, 3s below 3p below 3d, etc.	atomic-structure
What causes spin-orbit coupling and what is its energy correction?	In the electron's rest frame, the nucleus orbits the electron, creating a magnetic field B ∝ L. The spin magnetic moment couples: Ĥ_SO = ξ(r) L̂·Ŝ, where ξ(r) = e²/(8πε₀m²c²) · (1/r)(dU/dr) > 0. Energy correction: ΔE_SO = ½⟨ξ(r)⟩ [J(J+1) − L(L+1) − S(S+1)] ℏ², where J = L + S. Note: L̂·Ŝ = ½(Ĵ² − L̂² − Ŝ²).	fine-structure
What are the fine structure corrections to hydrogen and their relative magnitudes?	Fine structure = relativistic kinetic energy correction + spin-orbit coupling. Both are of order (Zα)² times the Bohr energy Eₙ ∼ α² mₑc²/n², where α = e²/(4πε₀ℏc) ≈ 1/137. They split levels by J quantum number. Result: Eₙⱼ = Eₙ[1 + (Zα/n)²(n/(j+½) − ¾/4)]. The Lamb shift (QED effect, ∼α³Eₙ) further splits states with the same j but different l.	fine-structure
What is hyperfine structure and what is the famous 21 cm line?	Hyperfine structure arises from the magnetic interaction between the nuclear magnetic moment (μ_I ∝ I) and the electronic angular momentum J: Ĥ_hf ∝ Î·Ĵ. The energy splitting is ∼(mₑ/mₚ) × fine structure ≈ 1000× smaller. For hydrogen ground state (1s): F = I+J = 0 (singlet) and F=1 (triplet) are split by ΔE = 5.87 μeV, corresponding to the 21 cm (1420 MHz) radio line used in astronomy.	fine-structure
Explain the Zeeman effect. Distinguish the normal, anomalous, and Paschen-Back regimes.	Normal Zeeman (S=0): ΔE = μ_B mₗ B — single triplet. Anomalous Zeeman (weak B, S≠0): L and S precess together about J; ΔE = g_J μ_B m_J B where Landé g_J = 1 + [J(J+1)+S(S+1)−L(L+1)]/[2J(J+1)]. Paschen-Back (strong B, μ_B B ≫ spin-orbit splitting): L and S precess independently; ΔE = μ_B(m_L + 2m_S)B.	fine-structure

#--- TIME-DEPENDENT PERTURBATION THEORY ---
State Fermi's Golden Rule.	For a constant perturbation Ĥ', the transition rate from state |i⟩ to a continuum of states near |f⟩ is: Γ_{i→f} = (2π/ℏ) |⟨f|Ĥ'|i⟩|² ρ(Ef), where ρ(Ef) is the density of final states at energy Ef = Ei. Derived from first-order TDPT in the long-time limit (t ≫ ℏ/ΔE). Applies when the perturbation is weak and transitions are irreversible.	time-dependent-PT
What is Rabi oscillation in a two-level system?	For a two-level system driven resonantly (ω = ω₀ = (E₂−E₁)/ℏ), the population of the upper state oscillates coherently: P₂(t) = sin²(Ωt/2), where Ω = |⟨2|Ĥ'|1⟩|/ℏ is the Rabi frequency. Off-resonance: P₂(t) = (Ω/Ω_eff)² sin²(Ω_eff t/2) with Ω_eff = √(Ω² + δ²), δ = ω − ω₀. Complete population inversion occurs at t = π/Ω (π-pulse).	time-dependent-PT
What are the electric dipole (E1) selection rules for atomic transitions?	From Fermi's Golden Rule, the rate is proportional to |⟨f|D̂|i⟩|² where D̂ = er is the dipole operator. Non-zero only if: Δl = ±1, Δm = 0, ±1, Δs = 0, and parity changes. These follow from the Wigner-Eckart theorem (D̂ is a rank-1 tensor under rotations) and the parity of spherical harmonics. Transitions violating these rules are "forbidden" (can occur via M1, E2, etc.).	time-dependent-PT
What is the interaction picture (Dirac picture)?	In the interaction picture: states evolve under Ĥ' alone, operators evolve under Ĥ₀. State: |ψ_I(t)⟩ = e^{iĤ₀t/ℏ}|ψ_S(t)⟩. Operator: Ô_I(t) = e^{iĤ₀t/ℏ}Ô_S e^{−iĤ₀t/ℏ}. Evolution: iℏ d|ψ_I⟩/dt = Ĥ'_I(t)|ψ_I⟩. The interaction picture separates the "fast" free evolution from the "slow" perturbative dynamics. It is the natural starting point for time-dependent perturbation theory.	time-dependent-PT
What is the dipole approximation in atom-light interaction?	When the photon wavelength λ ≫ atomic size a₀ (true for optical/UV photons where ka₀ ∼ α ≈ 1/137 ≪ 1), the spatial variation of the field across the atom is negligible: e^{ik·r} ≈ 1. The perturbation becomes Ĥ' ≈ −eE₀·r (electric dipole interaction). The next order gives magnetic dipole (M1) and electric quadrupole (E2) transitions, suppressed by (ka₀)² relative to E1.	time-dependent-PT
Contrast the sudden and adiabatic approximations.	Sudden approximation (τ → 0): Hamiltonian changes instantaneously. The wavefunction is unchanged; expand the old state in new eigenstates to find transition probabilities. Adiabatic approximation (τ → ∞): Hamiltonian changes infinitely slowly compared to ℏ/ΔE. The system remains in the instantaneous eigenstate (adiabatic theorem). Adiabatic condition: |⟨m|dĤ/dt|n⟩/((Eₙ−Eₘ)²/ℏ)| ≪ 1 for all m ≠ n.	time-dependent-PT

#--- SCATTERING THEORY ---
Define the differential cross-section and the scattering amplitude f(θ).	The asymptotic scattered wavefunction is ψ(r) → e^{ikz} + f(θ) e^{ikr}/r. The differential cross-section: dσ/dΩ = |f(θ,φ)|². Total cross-section: σ = ∫|f|²dΩ. Physical meaning: dσ is the effective area that scatters into solid angle dΩ. The incident flux is j_inc = ℏk/m; scattered flux is j_scat = (ℏk/m)|f|²/r².	scattering
State the Born approximation for the scattering amplitude.	f(θ) = −(m/2πℏ²) ∫ e^{−iq·r} V(r) d³r = −(m/2πℏ²) Ṽ(q), where q = k_f − k_i is the momentum transfer (|q| = 2k sin(θ/2)) and Ṽ(q) is the Fourier transform of V(r). Valid when |V| ≪ ℏ²k²/m (weak or high-energy scattering). For the Coulomb potential, the Born approximation yields the exact Rutherford formula.	scattering
What is partial wave analysis? State the key results.	For a spherically symmetric V(r), expand in partial waves with angular momentum l. Each wave acquires a real phase shift δₗ. Scattering amplitude: f(θ) = (1/k)Σₗ(2l+1)e^{iδₗ}sin(δₗ)Pₗ(cosθ). Total cross-section: σ = (4π/k²)Σₗ(2l+1)sin²δₗ. Maximum contribution of the l-th partial wave: σₗ^{max} = 4π(2l+1)/k² (unitarity limit).	scattering
State the optical theorem and explain its physical origin.	Im f(θ=0) = kσ_total/(4π). Physical origin: probability (flux) conservation. The total scattered power equals the reduction in forward flux (the "shadow" behind the target). The forward scattering amplitude must have an imaginary part accounting for the depletion of the incident beam by all scattering processes (elastic + inelastic).	scattering
What is the Lippmann-Schwinger equation?	|ψ+⟩ = |φ⟩ + (1/(E−Ĥ₀+iε)) V̂|ψ+⟩ = |φ⟩ + G₀⁺V|ψ+⟩. It is the exact integral equation for the scattering state, with outgoing (+iε) boundary conditions. Iterating generates the Born series: |ψ+⟩ = |φ⟩ + G₀⁺V|φ⟩ + G₀⁺VG₀⁺V|φ⟩ + ..., of which the Born approximation keeps only the first term.	scattering

#--- ELEMENTS OF QUANTUM FIELD THEORY ---
How is a quantum field theory constructed by canonical quantisation of a classical field?	(1) Start with field ϕ(x,t) with Lagrangian density L(ϕ, ∂ϕ). (2) Define canonical momentum density π(x,t) = ∂L/∂ϕ̇. (3) Impose equal-time commutation relations: [ϕ(x,t), π(x',t)] = iℏδ(x−x'). (4) Expand ϕ in normal modes; each mode is a quantum harmonic oscillator with ladder operators a_k, a_k†. This procedure ("second quantisation") creates and destroys quanta of the field.	QFT
What are phonons and how do they emerge from quantising the atomic chain?	An atomic chain with mass density ρ and stiffness κ supports classical sound waves (phonons) at frequency ω_k. Quantising the field gives quanta (phonons) with energy ℏω_k. The phonon creation operator a_k† adds one quantum of vibration to mode k. Phonons are bosons (symmetric states, integer occupation). Zero-point energy: Σ_k ½ℏω_k (vacuum fluctuations of the field).	QFT
What are photons and how do they emerge from quantising the EM field?	The vector potential A(r,t) is expanded in plane wave modes (polarisation λ, wavevector k). Each mode is a harmonic oscillator; quantising gives photons — quanta of the EM field with energy ℏω_k = ℏc|k|. Creation/annihilation operators a_{k,λ}†, a_{k,λ} with [a, a†] = 1. Photons are bosons with spin-1. The vacuum |0⟩ contains no photons but has zero-point fluctuations responsible for spontaneous emission and the Casimir effect.	QFT
What is spontaneous emission and how is its rate calculated?	An excited atom in vacuum spontaneously emits a photon due to coupling to the zero-point fluctuations of the quantised EM field. Rate (Einstein A coefficient) from Fermi's Golden Rule with photon density of states: A = e²ω³|⟨f|r̂|i⟩|² / (3πε₀ℏmₑc³). Stimulated emission rate = A·n̄ (n̄ = mean photon number). Ratio A/B_stimulated = ℏω³/(π²c³) (Einstein relation). Spontaneous emission is inherently quantum — no classical analogue.	QFT

#--- MOLECULAR STRUCTURE ---
What is the Born-Oppenheimer approximation?	Because nuclei are much heavier than electrons (M/mₑ ∼ 1000−100000), they move much more slowly. For fixed nuclear positions R, solve the electronic Schrödinger equation: Ĥ_el ψ_el = E_el(R) ψ_el. The electronic energy E_el(R) acts as the potential energy surface for nuclear motion. Valid when the electronic excitation gap ≫ vibrational energy spacing ℏω_vib ∼ (mₑ/M)^{½} E_el.	molecular-structure
What is the Heitler-London (valence bond) model of H₂? What does it give for the binding energy?	Trial wavefunction: ψ_VB ∝ [ψ_a(r₁)ψ_b(r₂) ± ψ_b(r₁)ψ_a(r₂)], where a,b label the two protons. Symmetric spatial (bonding): paired with singlet spin (S=0). Antisymmetric spatial (antibonding): paired with triplet spin (S=1). Variational calculation gives binding energy ≈ 3.14 eV (experimental: 4.75 eV). Ionic configurations (both electrons on same proton) can be added to improve agreement.	molecular-structure
What are bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals in the LCAO picture?	Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO): ψ± = (ψ_a ± ψ_b)/√(2(1±S)), where S = ⟨ψ_a|ψ_b⟩ is the overlap. ψ+ (bonding/σg): electron density concentrated between nuclei → lower energy. ψ₋ (antibonding/σu*): node between nuclei, electron density pulled away → higher energy. For H₂ ground state, both electrons occupy ψ+; for H₂⁺: one electron in ψ+.	molecular-structure

#--- ADDITIONAL KEY RESULTS ---
What is the de Broglie relation and what does it imply about matter?	p = ℏk = h/λ, or equivalently λ = h/p = h/√(2mE). All matter has an associated de Broglie wavelength. Quantum behaviour is prominent when λ is comparable to the system size. For electrons in atoms (p ∼ ℏ/a₀): λ ∼ a₀ — fully quantum. For macroscopic objects: λ ≪ system size → classical behaviour. Confirmed by electron diffraction (Davisson-Germer experiment).	foundations
What is quantum tunnelling and how does the WKB approximation describe it?	In classically forbidden regions (E < V), the wavefunction decays as ψ ∝ exp(−∫κ(x)dx) where κ(x) = √(2m(V−E))/ℏ. The WKB transmission probability: T ≈ exp(−2∫_{x₁}^{x₂}κ(x)dx). This gives: exponential sensitivity to barrier width and height. Applications: alpha decay (Gamow factor), tunnel diodes, scanning tunnelling microscopy, nuclear fusion.	perturbation-theory
State the virial theorem in quantum mechanics for power-law potentials.	For a stationary state: 2⟨T⟩ = ⟨r·∇V⟩. For V ∝ rⁿ: 2⟨T⟩ = n⟨V⟩. Key cases: Coulomb (n=−1): ⟨T⟩ = −½⟨V⟩ = −E (so E = ½⟨V⟩ = −⟨T⟩ < 0). Harmonic oscillator (n=2): ⟨T⟩ = ⟨V⟩ = E/2. Useful for computing expectation values without explicit integration.	formalism
What is the Hellmann-Feynman theorem?	If Ĥ(λ) depends on a parameter λ, and |n(λ)⟩ is a normalised eigenstate with energy Eₙ(λ), then: dEₙ/dλ = ⟨n(λ)|∂Ĥ/∂λ|n(λ)⟩. This avoids computing derivatives of eigenstates. Applications: ⟨1/r⟩, ⟨1/r²⟩ in hydrogen; deriving selection rules; proving the virial theorem. Requires that |n(λ)⟩ is non-degenerate (or variation is within a degenerate subspace).	formalism
What is the Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn (TRK) f-sum rule?	Σₙ fₙ₀ = 1 where fₙ₀ = (2mωₙ₀/ℏ)|⟨n|x̂|0⟩|² is the oscillator strength. Equivalently: Σₙ (Eₙ−E₀)|⟨n|x̂|0⟩|² = ℏ²/(2m). Derived from [x̂,[Ĥ,x̂]] = ℏ²/m and the completeness of eigenstates. It is an exact sum rule (no approximations) constraining the distribution of electric dipole transition strengths.	formalism
What is the density of states for a 3D free particle and why is it important?	g(E) = (V/2π²)(2m/ℏ²)^{3/2} √E = Vm√(2mE)/(π²ℏ³). It counts states per unit energy interval. Enters Fermi's Golden Rule: Γ = (2π/ℏ)|⟨f|V|i⟩|²g(Ef). Also essential for: free electron theory of metals, blackbody radiation (photon density of states ∝ ω²), and statistical mechanics of quantum gases.	formalism
What is the quantum defect model for multi-electron atoms?	For alkali atoms (one valence electron outside closed shells), inner electrons screen the nucleus. The valence electron sees an effective charge Z_eff, and the energy levels become Eₙₗ = −13.6 eV / (n − δₗ)², where δₗ is the quantum defect (largest for s-electrons, smallest for high-l). δₗ decreases with l because high-l electrons rarely penetrate the core (centrifugal barrier). This breaks the hydrogen-like l-degeneracy.	atomic-structure
What is the Thomas precession and why does it modify spin-orbit coupling?	The electron moves relativistically, and its rest frame is non-inertial (accelerating). The Thomas precession is a relativistic kinematic effect that reduces the spin-orbit interaction by a factor of 2: the full interaction is Ĥ_SO = (1/2mₑ²c²) · (1/r)(dV/dr) · L̂·Ŝ. The factor of ½ (Thomas factor) arises from the Lorentz transformation between electron and lab frames. Without it, spin-orbit would predict splittings twice too large.	fine-structure
What is the exchange-correlation energy and why does Hartree-Fock miss it?	Hartree-Fock captures the exchange energy (arising from antisymmetry of the wavefunction) but misses electron correlation: the additional energy reduction when electrons avoid each other beyond the mean-field description. Correlation energy = E_exact − E_HF < 0. For atoms, E_corr ∼ 1 eV per electron pair — small but chemically crucial. Density Functional Theory (DFT) attempts to include correlation via an exchange-correlation functional.	atomic-structure
What is the connection between the quantised EM field, stimulated emission, and Einstein's A and B coefficients?	Einstein's rate equations: dN₂/dt = −AN₂ − B₂₁ρ(ω)N₂ + B₁₂ρ(ω)N₁. At thermal equilibrium (Boltzmann) and using Planck's law: A/B = ℏω³/(π²c³) and B₁₂ = B₂₁ (for non-degenerate levels). Quantum field theory gives: B ∝ |⟨1|D̂|2⟩|² (Fermi's Golden Rule) and the A coefficient is the spontaneous emission rate arising from vacuum fluctuations. These relations link the classical radiation theory to QFT.	QFT
