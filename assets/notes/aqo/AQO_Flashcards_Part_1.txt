What is the paraxial approximation, and when is it valid?	The approximation sin θ ≈ θ ≈ tan θ for rays travelling at small angles to the optical axis. Valid when θ ≪ 1 rad. Underpins ray-transfer matrix optics.	geometric_optics
Focal length of a spherical mirror in the paraxial approximation	f = R/2, where R is the radius of curvature. Derived from geometric construction; fails outside paraxial regime (spherical aberration).	geometric_optics
Ray-transfer matrix for free-space propagation distance d	(1, d; 0, 1) — top row gives output position y' = y + θd, bottom row gives output angle θ' = θ.	geometric_optics
Ray-transfer matrix for a thin lens of focal length f	(1, 0; -1/f, 1) — position unchanged at the lens, angle deflected by -y/f.	geometric_optics
Ray-transfer matrix for refraction at a spherical interface (n₁→n₂, radius R)	(1, 0; (n₁-n₂)/(n₂R), n₁/n₂)	geometric_optics
Snell's law	n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, with angles measured from the normal to the interface.	geometric_optics
Critical angle for total internal reflection	θ_c = sin⁻¹(n₂/n₁), valid only when n₁ > n₂. Light incident from the denser medium at θ ≥ θ_c is totally reflected.	geometric_optics
Wave equation (scalar wave optics)	∇²u − (1/c²) ∂²u/∂t² = 0, where u(r,t) is the real scalar wavefunction and c = c₀/n.	wave_optics
Helmholtz equation	∇²U + k²U = 0, where U(r) is the complex amplitude and k = 2πν/c is the wavenumber. Obtained from the wave equation for monochromatic light U(r,t) = U(r)exp(j2πνt).	wave_optics
Optical intensity in terms of complex amplitude	I(r) = |U(r)|². For monochromatic light this is time-independent. Time average of 2u² gives the same result.	wave_optics
Definition of wavefronts	Surfaces of constant phase φ(r) = 2πq. The wavefront normal ∇φ gives the direction of propagation.	wave_optics
Complex degree of temporal coherence g(τ)	g(τ) = ⟨U*(t)U(t+τ)⟩ / ⟨|U(t)|²⟩. Satisfies 0 ≤ |g(τ)| ≤ 1; quantifies how correlated the field is with itself after delay τ.	coherence
Coherence time τ_c (power-equivalent width definition)	τ_c = ∫₋∞^∞ |g(τ)|² dτ. Coherence length: l_c = c·τ_c.	coherence
Coherence time vs. spectral width relation	τ_c ≈ 1/Δν (order of magnitude). Lorentzian: τ_c = 1/(πΔν). Gaussian: τ_c = √(2 ln 2/π)/Δν.	coherence
|g(τ)| for an exponential (Lorentzian spectrum)	|g(τ)| = exp(-|τ|/τ_c). Corresponds to a Lorentzian power spectrum of FWHM Δν = 1/(πτ_c).	coherence
|g(τ)| for a Gaussian spectrum	|g(τ)| = exp(-πτ²/(2τ_c²)). Corresponds to a Gaussian power spectrum.	coherence
Two-wave interference intensity formula	I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂) Re[g(τ)] = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂)|g(τ)|cos(2π ν̄ τ + arg g). For equal intensities: I = 2I₀[1 + |g(τ)|cos(2π ν̄ τ)].	coherence
Fringe visibility	𝒱 = (I_max − I_min)/(I_max + I_min) = 2√(I₁I₂)|g(τ)|/(I₁+I₂). For equal intensities, 𝒱 = |g(τ)|.	coherence
Gaussian beam complex amplitude	U(r) = (A₀ W₀/W(z)) exp(-ρ²/W²(z)) exp(-jkz - jkρ²/(2R(z)) + jζ(z)), where ρ = √(x²+y²).	beam_optics
Gaussian beam width W(z)	W(z) = W₀ √(1 + (z/z₀)²). At z = z₀, W = √2 W₀.	beam_optics
Gaussian beam Rayleigh range z₀	z₀ = πW₀²/λ. Sets the longitudinal scale over which the beam stays roughly collimated.	beam_optics
Gaussian beam wavefront radius R(z)	R(z) = z[1 + (z₀/z)²]. R → ∞ at z = 0 (planar) and z → ∞ (spherical wave).	beam_optics
Gaussian beam divergence half-angle	θ₀ = λ/(πW₀) = W₀/z₀ (far-field). Smaller waists diverge faster — diffraction limit.	beam_optics
Gouy phase ζ(z)	ζ(z) = tan⁻¹(z/z₀). Extra π phase shift accumulated by a Gaussian beam compared to a plane wave on traversing the focus.	beam_optics
Beam parameter q(z)	q(z) = z + jz₀; equivalently 1/q = 1/R - jλ/(πW²). Linear in z; transforms by ABCD law: q' = (Aq+B)/(Cq+D).	beam_optics
M² beam-quality factor	M² = (2W_m · 2θ_m)/(4λ/π). Ideal Gaussian: M² = 1. Real beams M² ≥ 1.	beam_optics
Focused waist of a Gaussian beam (z₀ ≫ f limit)	W₀' ≈ fλ/(πW₀). Lens at z = 0 with incoming collimated beam of waist W₀.	beam_optics
Plane-wave decomposition (angular spectrum)	Any field f(x,y) at z=0 can be written as a 2D Fourier integral over plane waves with transverse spatial frequencies (νₓ, ν_y), where (kₓ, k_y) = 2π(νₓ, ν_y).	fourier_optics
Spatial frequency / angle relation	θ_x ≈ λν_x in the paraxial regime. Spatial frequencies map directly to propagation angles.	fourier_optics
Transfer function of free space	H(ν_x, ν_y) = exp(-jk_z d), with k_z = √(k² - k_x² - k_y²) = 2π√(λ⁻² - ν_x² - ν_y²).	fourier_optics
Fresnel approximation transfer function	H(ν_x, ν_y) ≈ H₀ exp[jπλd(ν_x² + ν_y²)], where H₀ = exp(-jkd). Valid in the near field, paraxial regime.	fourier_optics
Fresnel impulse response (free-space propagator)	h(x,y) = (h₀/jλd) exp[-jk(x²+y²)/(2d)], h₀ = exp(-jkd). Convolving with input field f(x,y) gives output g(x,y).	fourier_optics
Fraunhofer (far-field) diffraction	g(x,y) ∝ F(x/(λd), y/(λd)) — output is the Fourier transform of the aperture, evaluated at spatial frequencies (x/(λd), y/(λd)). Valid for d ≫ a²/λ.	fourier_optics
Lens as Fourier transformer	A thin lens of focal length f produces, at its back focal plane, the Fourier transform of the field at its front focal plane: g(x,y) ∝ F(x/(λf), y/(λf)).	fourier_optics
4f imaging system	Two lenses (f₁, f₂) separated by f₁+f₂; object at front focal plane of L₁, image at back focal plane of L₂. Lateral magnification m = -f₂/f₁. Ray matrix is diagonal (perfect imaging).	fourier_optics
Diffraction-limited resolution (Abbe / Rayleigh)	Δx_min ≈ 0.61 λ/NA (Rayleigh criterion). Minimum resolvable separation for an imaging system of numerical aperture NA.	fourier_optics
Confocal microscopy principle	A pinhole at the conjugate image plane rejects out-of-focus light. Improves axial resolution and sectioning compared to widefield microscopy.	microscopy
STED superresolution principle	Stimulated emission depletion: a doughnut-shaped depletion beam suppresses fluorescence except at the very centre, beating the diffraction limit. Resolution scales as ~λ/(2NA·√(1+I/I_sat)).	microscopy
STORM/PALM principle	Stochastic activation and localisation of single fluorophores. Centroid fitting localises each emitter to ~nm precision; image built up from many sparse frames.	microscopy
Origin of nonlinear polarisation	Anharmonic oscillator: V = ½k₀x² + ⅓k₁x³ + ¼k₂x⁴+… The cubic and higher terms produce displacement components at harmonics of the driving frequency, giving P(E) = ε₀(χ⁽¹⁾E + 2dE² + 4χ⁽³⁾E³ + …).	nonlinear_optics
Why χ⁽²⁾ vanishes in centrosymmetric media	Inversion symmetry requires P(-E) = -P(E), forbidding all even powers of E. So χ⁽²⁾ = χ⁽⁴⁾ = … = 0. χ⁽³⁾ can still be nonzero.	nonlinear_optics
Frequency components in three-wave mixing	For E(t) = Re[E(ω₁)e^(iω₁t) + E(ω₂)e^(iω₂t)], P⁽²⁾ contains components at: 0 (rectification), 2ω₁, 2ω₂ (SHG), ω₁+ω₂ (SFG), ω₁-ω₂ (DFG).	nonlinear_optics
Frequency-matching condition	ω₃ = ω₁ + ω₂ — conservation of energy for the photons involved.	nonlinear_optics
Phase-matching condition	k₁ + k₂ = k₃ — conservation of momentum. Required for sustained energy transfer over the crystal length; otherwise the generated wave dephases and oscillates.	nonlinear_optics
Coherence length for nonlinear conversion	l_c = π/|Δk|, where Δk = k₁ + k₂ - k₃. Beyond this length, the generated power oscillates instead of growing.	nonlinear_optics
Type-I birefringent phase matching	Both fundamental photons have the same (ordinary) polarisation; the generated photon has the orthogonal (extraordinary) polarisation. Phase matching achieved by tuning the propagation angle θ relative to the optic axis.	nonlinear_optics
Manley–Rowe relations	d(I₁/ω₁)/dz = d(I₂/ω₂)/dz = -d(I₃/ω₃)/dz. Equivalent to photon-number conservation: photons at ω₃ are created at the rate at which photons at ω₁ and ω₂ are destroyed.	nonlinear_optics
Pockels effect (linear electro-optic)	χ⁽²⁾(ω;ω,0): a DC field E₀ modifies the refractive index linearly: n(E₀) ≈ n₀ + (χ⁽²⁾/(2n₀))E₀. Only in non-centrosymmetric crystals.	nonlinear_optics
Half-wave voltage V_π of a Pockels cell	V_π is the voltage required for the two principal polarisations to acquire a relative phase shift of π. Between crossed polarisers: I_out = I_in sin²(πV/(2V_π)).	nonlinear_optics
Optical Kerr effect	χ⁽³⁾(ω;ω,ω,-ω): refractive index depends on intensity, n(I) = n₀ + n₂I, where n₂ = χ⁽³⁾/(2n₀²ε₀c). Allowed in any medium (including centrosymmetric).	nonlinear_optics
Self-phase modulation	A pulse passing through a Kerr medium acquires a time-dependent phase φ(t) = ω₀t - k₀z[n₀ + n₂I(t)], so the instantaneous frequency ω(t) = ω₀ - k₀z·n₂·dI/dt is chirped: red leading edge, blue trailing edge.	nonlinear_optics
Self-focusing in a Kerr medium	A Gaussian intensity profile produces a parabolic refractive-index profile (highest on axis), acting as a positive lens of focal length f_K = w²/(4n₂I₀d) for a slab of thickness d.	nonlinear_optics
Critical power for self-focusing	P_cr ≈ αλ²/(4πn₀n₂), with α ≈ 1.9 (Chiao-Garmire-Townes). Beam collapses if P > P_cr.	nonlinear_optics
Optical soliton	Solution of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation in which Kerr self-phase modulation exactly balances anomalous group-velocity dispersion. Profile: A(x,z) = A₀ sech(x/W₀) exp(-jz/(4z₀)).	nonlinear_optics
Group velocity dispersion (GVD)	GVD = ∂²k/∂ω² = (1/c)·(2 ∂n/∂ω + ω ∂²n/∂ω²). Units: time²/distance. Anomalous GVD < 0; normal GVD > 0.	nonlinear_optics
Acousto-optic Bragg condition	2Λ sin θ_B = λ/n, where Λ is the acoustic wavelength and θ_B the Bragg angle inside the medium. Diffracted beam is frequency-shifted by ±f_acoustic.	nonlinear_optics
Three modes of light-matter interaction	(1) Spontaneous emission: P_sp = (c/V)σ(ν), independent of photon number. (2) Stimulated emission: P_st = n(c/V)σ(ν). (3) Absorption: P_ab = n(c/V)σ(ν). Stimulated rates equal absorption rates per atom.	lasers
Transition cross section σ(ν)	Effective area presented by an atom for absorption/stimulated emission. Related to spontaneous lifetime by σ(ν) = (λ²/(8πt_sp))g(ν), where g(ν) is the normalised lineshape.	lasers
Pumping rate equations (no radiation)	dN₂/dt = R₂ - N₂/τ₂; dN₁/dt = -R₁ - N₁/τ₁ + N₂/τ₂₁. Steady-state population inversion: N₀ = R₂τ₂(1 - τ₁/τ₂₁) + R₁τ₁.	lasers
Conditions for large population inversion	(1) Large pumping rates R₁, R₂. (2) Long upper-state lifetime τ₂. (3) Short lower-state lifetime τ₁ ≪ τ₂₁. (4) τ₂₁ ≈ t_sp ≪ τ₂₀ (radiative decay dominates).	lasers
Rate equations with radiation	Add the term ±(N₂-N₁)W_i to each, where W_i = φσ(ν) is the stimulated transition rate. Steady-state solution: N = N₀/(1 + τ_s W_i).	lasers
Saturation time constant	τ_s = τ₂ + τ₁(1 - τ₂/τ₂₁). Always positive. Sets the photon-flux density at which the gain saturates: φ_s(ν) = 1/(τ_s σ(ν)).	lasers
Saturated gain coefficient	γ(ν) = γ₀(ν)/(1 + φ/φ_s(ν)). γ₀ = N₀σ is the small-signal (unsaturated) gain.	lasers
Two conditions for laser oscillation	(1) Gain condition: γ₀(ν) > α_r — round-trip gain exceeds round-trip loss. (2) Phase condition: 2kd + 2φ(ν)d = 2πq — round-trip phase is an integer multiple of 2π.	lasers
Total round-trip loss coefficient α_r	α_r = α_s + α_m1 + α_m2, where α_mi = (1/(2d))ln(1/Rᵢ) is the loss coefficient associated with mirror i.	lasers
Photon lifetime τ_p	τ_p = 1/(α_r c). The 1/e decay time of light intensity in an empty (unpumped) cavity. Higher loss → shorter τ_p → broader resonator linewidth.	lasers
Threshold population inversion	N_t = α_r/σ(ν) = 1/(cτ_p σ(ν)). The minimum inversion needed for the gain to overcome the round-trip loss.	lasers
Steady-state laser photon flux above threshold	φ = φ_s(ν)[γ₀(ν)/α_r - 1] for γ₀ > α_r; φ = 0 below threshold. Output flux is φ × (α_m/α_r).	lasers
Gain clamping	Above threshold, the saturated gain is clamped to equal the loss: γ(ν) = α_r. Any further increase in pumping shows up as more output power, not more gain.	lasers
Resonance frequencies of a Fabry–Perot resonator	ν_q = q·c/(2nd), q = 1, 2, 3,... Free spectral range: ν_F = c/(2nd).	resonators
Finesse of a Fabry–Perot resonator	ℱ = π√|r|/(1-|r|), where |r|² is the round-trip intensity attenuation. For low losses, ℱ ≈ π/(α_r d).	resonators
Spectral width of resonator modes	δν ≈ ν_F/ℱ. Higher finesse → sharper resonances. Quality factor Q = ν₀/δν.	resonators
Airy function for resonator transmission	I/I_max = 1/[1 + (2ℱ/π)² sin²(φ/2)], where φ = 2kd is the round-trip phase. Sharply peaked at resonances for high ℱ.	resonators
Stability condition for spherical-mirror resonator	0 ≤ g₁g₂ ≤ 1, where gᵢ = 1 - d/Rᵢ. Beyond this region the beam diverges away with each round trip.	resonators
Special spherical-mirror resonator configurations	Planar: g₁=g₂=1 (R=∞). Confocal: g₁=g₂=0 (R=d). Concentric: g₁=g₂=-1 (R=d/2). Symmetric: g₁=g₂.	resonators
Number of laser longitudinal modes	M ≈ B/ν_F, where B is the spectral width over which γ₀ > α_r. Homogeneously broadened: ultimately one mode (single-mode operation). Inhomogeneously broadened: multimode possible due to spectral hole burning.	lasers
Spectral hole burning	A strong intracavity field at one frequency selectively saturates the gain of the atoms resonant at that frequency, creating a localised dip in the gain profile. Allows simultaneous oscillation of multiple modes in inhomogeneously broadened lasers.	lasers
Schawlow–Townes laser linewidth	Δν_l ~ 1/(τ_p N_ph). Quantum-limited linewidth from random phase kicks due to spontaneous emission. Improves with high power and high Q.	lasers
Mode locking principle	Lock the relative phases of many longitudinal modes so they interfere constructively at periodic intervals. Produces a train of ultrashort pulses with repetition period 2d/c and pulse duration ~1/(M·ν_F) for M locked modes.	lasers
Frequency comb	Output of a mode-locked laser: a series of equally spaced spectral lines at frequencies ν_n = n·f_rep + f_CEO, where f_rep = c/(2d) is the repetition rate and f_CEO is the carrier-envelope offset. Foundation of optical clocks.	lasers
Self-consistency condition for planar mirror waveguide	(2π/λ)·2d sin θ = 2πm. Bounce angles: sin θ_m = mλ/(2d), m = 1, 2, 3,…	guided_optics
Propagation constant in a planar mirror waveguide	β_m² = k² - m²π²/d², so β_m = k cos θ_m. Higher-order modes have smaller β (slower along z).	guided_optics
Self-consistency for dielectric planar waveguide	(2π/λ)·2d sin θ - 2φ_r = 2πm, where φ_r is the phase shift from total internal reflection. m = 0, 1, 2,…	guided_optics
Numerical aperture of a planar/fibre waveguide	NA = √(n₁² - n₂²). Equals sin of the half-angle of the acceptance cone in air.	guided_optics
Number of TE modes in a dielectric slab waveguide	M ≈ ⌈2d·NA/λ₀⌉. There is no cut-off for the m=0 mode in a symmetric waveguide; it always propagates.	guided_optics
Single-mode condition (planar dielectric)	ν > ν_c = c₀/(2d·NA), or equivalently 2d·NA/λ₀ < 1. The fundamental m=0 mode always propagates; cut-offs apply to higher modes only.	guided_optics
Step-index fibre V-parameter	V = (2πa/λ₀)·NA, where a is the core radius. Single-mode fibre when V < 2.405 (first zero of J₀).	guided_optics
Modal dispersion in multimode fibres	Different transverse modes have different group velocities, broadening pulses. Overcome by using single-mode fibre or graded-index profiles.	guided_optics
Quantum optics levels of description	Ray < wave < electromagnetic < quantum. Ray: λ→0 limit. Wave: scalar U(r,t). EM: full Maxwell with vector E, B and polarisation. Quantum: photons, field operators, coherent/squeezed states.	overview
Beat frequency in optical heterodyning	Mixing two waves at ν₁, ν₂ on a square-law detector gives I(t) = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂)cos[2π(ν₂-ν₁)t]. Used for sensitive frequency/velocity measurement.	coherence
Doppler shift in optical heterodyning	Reflected wave from object moving at velocity v: Δν = ±(2v/c)ν₀. Basis of coherent lidar and laser Doppler vibrometry.	coherence
