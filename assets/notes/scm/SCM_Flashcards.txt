What defines soft matter?	Materials readily deformed by thermal fluctuations or mechanical stresses. The elastic moduli are small enough that k<sub>B</sub>T at room temperature is sufficient to cause significant deformation or rearrangement.	soft_matter ch1_introduction
Why is the elastic modulus E a useful indicator of softness?	E has units of pressure (energy density). For a given applied pressure p, the strain is ε = p/E. Soft materials have E in the kPa–MPa range (e.g. gels ~10³ Pa, rubber ~10⁷ Pa) while hard materials are 10¹⁰–10¹¹ Pa (diamond, metals).	soft_matter ch1_introduction
Give three examples of soft matter systems.	Polymers (plastics, rubber), colloidal suspensions (paint, milk, blood), surfactants and amphiphiles (soap, lipid membranes), liquid crystals, gels, foams, granular media, biological tissues.	soft_matter ch1_introduction
State the continuity equation for a compressible fluid.	∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρv) = 0<br><br>This expresses conservation of mass: the rate of change of density at a point equals minus the divergence of the mass flux ρv.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
How does the continuity equation simplify for an incompressible fluid?	∇·v = 0<br><br>Since ρ is constant, the velocity field must be divergence-free.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
What is the stress tensor σ<sub>ij</sub>?	The force per unit area in the i-direction transmitted across a surface whose normal points in the j-direction. dF<sub>i</sub> = σ<sub>ij</sub> n<sub>j</sub> dS.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
State the viscous stress tensor for a Newtonian fluid in general geometry.	σ'<sub>ij</sub> = η(∂<sub>i</sub>v<sub>j</sub> + ∂<sub>j</sub>v<sub>i</sub>)<br><br>The viscous stress is proportional to the symmetric part of the velocity gradient. The proportionality constant η is the viscosity.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
Write the Navier–Stokes equation for an incompressible Newtonian fluid.	ρ[∂<sub>t</sub>v + (v·∇)v] = −∇p + η∇²v + ρg<br><br>Left side: inertial force densities (local + convective acceleration). Right side: pressure gradient, viscous force, body force.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
What is the material (convective) derivative?	Df/Dt = ∂f/∂t + (v·∇)f<br><br>It gives the rate of change of a quantity f as you follow a fluid element. The first term is the local (Eulerian) rate of change; the second is the convective contribution from moving with the flow.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
Define the Reynolds number and state its physical meaning.	Re = ρv₀L₀/η<br><br>It is the dimensionless ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces. Re ≪ 1 → viscous flow dominates (Stokes regime); Re ≫ 1 → inertia dominates (turbulent regime).	soft_matter ch2_fluids
State the Stokes equation (low-Reynolds-number flow).	0 = −∇p + η∇²v<br><br>Obtained by neglecting both the inertial term and the time derivative in the Navier–Stokes equation when Re ≪ 1.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
State the time-dependent Stokes equation.	ρ∂<sub>t</sub>v = −∇p + η∇²v<br><br>Used when external driving (e.g. oscillating boundary) sets the timescale rather than the intrinsic flow timescale.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
What are the four key properties of Stokes flow?	(1) Instantaneous: no time derivative, so no flow inertia. (2) Linear: doubling the pressure doubles the velocity field. (3) Reversible: reversing forces reverses flow. (4) Streamlines are unchanged before and after passage of an object.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
State the Scallop theorem.	At low Reynolds number, a swimmer that performs a reciprocal motion (a sequence of shape changes that retraces itself in reverse) cannot achieve net translation. This is because Stokes flow is kinematically reversible.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
How do microorganisms break the constraints of the Scallop theorem?	By adopting non-reciprocal swimming strategies: rotating helical flagella (e.g. E. coli), or beating cilia/flagella with a power stroke and recovery stroke that follow different paths in configuration space.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
Define vorticity and state its evolution equation in Stokes flow.	Vorticity: ω = ∇×v.<br><br>Evolution: ∂<sub>t</sub>ω = (η/ρ)∇²ω<br><br>This is a diffusion equation with kinematic viscosity ν = η/ρ as the diffusion coefficient — momentum diffuses through the fluid.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
State the Stokes drag force on a sphere.	F = 6πηav<br><br>Where a is the sphere radius, η the fluid viscosity, and v the sphere velocity relative to the fluid. Valid in the low-Reynolds-number regime, Re = ρva/η ≪ 1.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
Why does the Stokes drag force scale as ~a (radius), not ~a² (cross-section)?	Because the drag is dominated by momentum diffusion through the fluid, not by collisions with solvent molecules. The flow field around a moving sphere decays as v(r) ~ va/r, and integrating viscous stress over the sphere surface gives F ∝ ηav. Kinetic theory would predict F ∝ a², which is incorrect.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
Solve the Couette flow profile between parallel plates.	Bottom plate at z=0 stationary, top plate at z=h moving with velocity v₀ in x-direction. The Stokes equation reduces to η∂²v<sub>x</sub>/∂z² = 0, giving a linear velocity profile:<br><br>v<sub>x</sub>(z) = v₀ z/h	soft_matter ch2_fluids
How is Couette flow used to measure viscosity?	Measure the force F<sub>x</sub> = ηv₀A/h required to maintain the top plate moving at velocity v₀, where A is the plate area and h the gap. Solving for η gives the viscosity.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
For oscillatory flow near a wall (frequency ω), what is the penetration depth δ?	δ = √(2η/(ωρ))<br><br>The flow decays exponentially away from the wall with this characteristic length. Higher frequency or higher density → smaller penetration depth.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
What is the Oseen tensor for hydrodynamic interactions?	H<sub>nm</sub> = (1/(8πη|r<sub>nm</sub>|)) [I + r̂<sub>nm</sub>r̂<sub>nm</sub>]<br><br>It gives the velocity of particle n induced by a point force at particle m (n ≠ m). The 1/r decay means hydrodynamic interactions are very long-ranged.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
Why are hydrodynamic interactions called long-ranged?	The flow field around a moving sphere decays as 1/r (much slower than typical interparticle forces). A particle moving in a fluid drags neighbouring particles even when they are far away.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
State the Poiseuille flow profile in a circular pipe of radius a.	v<sub>x</sub>(r) = (Δp/(4ηL))(a² − r²)<br><br>A parabolic profile with maximum velocity at the centre and zero at the walls (no-slip).	soft_matter ch2_fluids
What is the volumetric flow rate in Poiseuille flow through a circular pipe?	Q = πa⁴Δp/(8ηL)<br><br>Note the strong fourth-power dependence on radius — halving the radius reduces flow by a factor of 16.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
State the Hagen–Poiseuille law and define hydraulic resistance.	Δp = R<sub>hydr</sub>Q<br><br>Hydraulic resistance is the proportionality constant. For a circular pipe: R<sub>hydr</sub> = 8ηL/(πa⁴). This is mathematically identical to Ohm's law ΔV = RI.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
Define hydraulic capacitance (compliance).	C<sub>hydr</sub> = −dV/dp<br><br>The analogue of electrical capacitance. Even 'incompressible' fluids in real channels have compliance because the channels themselves deform under pressure.	soft_matter ch2_fluids
Define the strain tensor for small deformations.	ε<sub>ik</sub> = ½(∂u<sub>i</sub>/∂x<sub>k</sub> + ∂u<sub>k</sub>/∂x<sub>i</sub>)<br><br>It is the symmetric part of the displacement gradient. The displacement field is u = x' − x where x and x' are the undeformed and deformed coordinates.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
Why must the strain tensor be symmetric?	Because the antisymmetric part of the displacement gradient ∂<sub>k</sub>u<sub>i</sub> − ∂<sub>i</sub>u<sub>k</sub> represents a rigid-body rotation, which doesn't deform the body. Only the symmetric part captures genuine deformation.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
How is volume change related to the strain tensor?	ΔV/V = Tr(ε) = ε<sub>ii</sub> = ∇·u<br><br>The trace of the strain tensor gives the relative volume change. For incompressible deformations (e.g. shear), Tr(ε) = 0.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
State Hooke's law in tensor form for an isotropic material.	σ = λ Tr(ε) I + 2Gε<br><br>or in index notation: σ<sub>ij</sub> = λε<sub>kk</sub>δ<sub>ij</sub> + 2Gε<sub>ij</sub><br><br>where λ and G are the Lamé coefficients. Only two independent elastic constants describe an isotropic material.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
Define Young's modulus E and Poisson's ratio ν.	Apply uniaxial stress σ<sub>xx</sub>:<br>• Young's modulus: E = σ<sub>xx</sub>/ε<sub>xx</sub><br>• Poisson's ratio: ν = −ε<sub>yy</sub>/ε<sub>xx</sub><br><br>Poisson's ratio measures how much a material contracts laterally when stretched.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
Define the bulk modulus B.	B = −V (∂p/∂V) = E/(3(1−2ν))<br><br>Resists volume change under hydrostatic pressure. p = −B(ΔV/V).	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
Define the shear modulus G and relate it to E and ν.	σ<sub>xy</sub> = 2Gε<sub>xy</sub><br><br>G = E/(2(1+ν))<br><br>For an incompressible material (ν = 1/2), G = E/3.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
Why must Poisson's ratio satisfy ν < 1/2?	Because the bulk modulus B = E/(3(1−2ν)) must be positive — a negative bulk modulus would mean the material expands when compressed, leading to runaway instability. Setting ν = 1/2 gives B → ∞ (incompressible).	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
Why must the shear modulus be positive?	If G < 0, the material would deform in the opposite direction to an applied shear stress (pulling away from the force) — unstable, runaway behaviour.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
What is an auxetic material?	A material with negative Poisson's ratio (ν < 0): it expands laterally when stretched longitudinally. Most natural materials have ν > 0, but artificial structures with suitable microarchitecture (e.g. miniaturised umbrella mechanisms) can be auxetic.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
What is linear viscoelasticity?	A regime where the stress response is linearly proportional to the applied strain (or strain rate), and the shear modulus is independent of strain magnitude. The response to different perturbations is additive (Boltzmann superposition principle). Valid for sufficiently small deformations.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
Describe the three standard rheological experiments.	(1) Creep: apply constant stress σ₀, measure strain ε(t). Compliance Γ(t) = ε(t)/σ₀.<br>(2) Stress relaxation: apply constant strain ε₀, measure stress σ(t). Relaxation modulus G(t) = σ(t)/ε₀.<br>(3) Oscillatory: apply oscillating strain (or stress), measure the other; gives complex modulus G*(ω).	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
State the Boltzmann superposition principle for linear viscoelasticity.	σ(t) = ∫<sup>t</sup><sub>−∞</sub> G(t−t') (dε/dt') dt'<br><br>The stress at time t is a linear superposition of contributions from all past strain increments, weighted by the relaxation modulus G(t−t').	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
State the Maxwell model constitutive equation.	dε/dt = (1/G₀)(dσ/dt) + σ/η<br><br>Equivalent to a spring (modulus G₀) and dashpot (viscosity η) in series. Defines a single relaxation time τ = η/G₀.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
Derive the Maxwell relaxation modulus G(t).	In stress relaxation, dε/dt = 0, so:<br>0 = (1/G₀)(dσ/dt) + σ/η ⟹ dσ/dt = −σ/τ<br><br>Solution: σ(t) = G₀ε₀ exp(−t/τ)<br><br>So G(t) = G₀ exp(−t/τ) with τ = η/G₀.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
Describe the short-time and long-time behaviour of the Maxwell model.	Short times (t ≪ τ): G(t) ≈ G₀, behaves like an elastic solid with shear modulus G₀.<br>Long times (t ≫ τ): G(t) → 0, behaves like a Newtonian fluid (flows). Crossover time is τ = η/G₀.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
State the complex modulus G*(ω) for the Maxwell model.	G*(ω) = iωG₀η/(G₀ + iωη)<br><br>Splitting into real and imaginary parts:<br>• G'(ω) = G₀ω²τ²/(1+ω²τ²)<br>• G''(ω) = G₀ωτ/(1+ω²τ²)<br><br>where τ = η/G₀.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
What do the storage modulus G' and loss modulus G'' represent?	G' (storage): in-phase elastic response, captures energy stored elastically per cycle.<br>G'' (loss): out-of-phase viscous response, captures energy dissipated per cycle.<br><br>G* = G' + iG''	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
At what frequency does G' = G'' in the Maxwell model?	When ωτ = 1 (i.e. ω = 1/τ = G₀/η).<br><br>At this crossover, G' = G'' = G₀/2. Below this, G'' dominates (liquid-like); above this, G' dominates (solid-like).	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
What is the Kelvin–Voigt model?	A spring (G₀) and dashpot (η) in parallel. Strain is the same in both: ε = ε<sub>spring</sub> = ε<sub>dashpot</sub>. Total stress σ = G₀ε + η(dε/dt). Exhibits viscoelastic creep ε(t) = (σ₀/G₀)(1 − e<sup>−t/τ</sup>) but no stress relaxation.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
What is the Zener (standard linear solid) model?	A Maxwell element (spring G₂ in series with dashpot η) connected in parallel with a second spring G₁. Exhibits both stress relaxation (incompletely — to a finite value G₁ε₀) and creep. Has two characteristic time scales.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
State the Langevin equation.	m(dv/dt) = −γv + ξ(t)<br><br>where γ is the friction coefficient (= 6πηa for a sphere) and ξ(t) is a stochastic random force describing molecular collisions with the solvent.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
What are the statistical properties of the white-noise random force ξ(t)?	⟨ξ(t)⟩ = 0 (no preferred direction)<br><br>⟨ξ(t)·ξ(t')⟩ = 6γk<sub>B</sub>T δ(t−t')<br><br>The amplitude is fixed by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
State the fluctuation-dissipation theorem (for the Langevin equation).	⟨ξ(t)·ξ(t')⟩ = 6γk<sub>B</sub>T δ(t−t')<br><br>The amplitude of thermal fluctuations of the random force is determined by the dissipative friction γ and the temperature. It links spontaneous fluctuations to dissipation.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
What is the long-time mean-square displacement of a Brownian particle?	⟨x²(t)⟩ = 6Dt<br><br>(in 3D; ⟨x²⟩ = 2Dt in 1D) — characteristic linear-in-time growth of diffusive motion.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
State the Einstein relation for the diffusion coefficient.	D = k<sub>B</sub>T/γ<br><br>This relates diffusion (a fluctuation property) to friction (a dissipation property) — another form of the fluctuation-dissipation theorem.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
State the Stokes–Einstein relation.	D = k<sub>B</sub>T/(6πηa)<br><br>For a spherical particle of radius a in a fluid of viscosity η. Combines the Einstein relation with Stokes drag γ = 6πηa.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
What is the diffusion equation for the concentration of Brownian particles?	∂c/∂t = D∇²c<br><br>Equivalent to the partial differential equation governing the probability density of a random walker in the continuum limit.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
State Fick's law for diffusive flux.	J = −D∇c<br><br>Particle flux is proportional to (and opposite in direction to) the concentration gradient. Combined with the continuity equation ∂<sub>t</sub>c = −∇·J, this gives the diffusion equation.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
State the Smoluchowski rate constant for diffusion-controlled reactions.	k = 4πDa<br><br>This is the maximum possible rate constant for a reaction in solution — assumes every encounter between reactants leads to a reaction. Valid when a is the contact radius.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
What is the velocity relaxation time τ<sub>v</sub>?	τ<sub>v</sub> = m/γ<br><br>The timescale on which a Brownian particle 'forgets' its initial velocity due to friction. Of order 10⁻¹⁰–10⁻⁸ s for colloidal particles in water.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
What is the overdamped (Smoluchowski) limit of the Langevin equation?	γv = ξ(t)<br><br>(or equivalently γ(dx/dt) = ξ(t) + F<sub>ext</sub>)<br><br>Valid on timescales t ≫ m/γ, when inertia is negligible. Used for most colloidal/Brownian dynamics.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
For confined Brownian motion in a harmonic potential V = ½kx², what is ⟨x²⟩ at long times?	⟨x²⟩ = 3k<sub>B</sub>T/k<br><br>(in 3D) — given by equipartition theorem with each direction contributing k<sub>B</sub>T/2 of potential energy. In 1D, ⟨x²⟩ = k<sub>B</sub>T/k.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
What is the characteristic time τ<sub>x</sub> for a Brownian particle to relax to its equilibrium distribution in a harmonic trap?	τ<sub>x</sub> = γ/k<br><br>where k is the spring constant of the trap and γ the friction coefficient. The position correlation function decays as exp(−t/τ<sub>x</sub>).	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
State the Smoluchowski equation (Fokker–Planck for overdamped motion).	∂c/∂t = ∇·[D∇c + (1/γ)∇U(x) c]<br><br>Describes the time evolution of the probability distribution of an overdamped Brownian particle in an external potential U(x).	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
State the Kramers (Arrhenius) escape rate from a metastable well.	r = D √(κ<sub>A</sub>κ<sup>‡</sup>)/(2πk<sub>B</sub>T) × exp(−U<sup>‡</sup>/k<sub>B</sub>T)<br><br>where κ<sub>A</sub> is the curvature at the well minimum, κ<sup>‡</sup> at the barrier top, and U<sup>‡</sup> is the barrier height. Exponential dependence on barrier height (Arrhenius behaviour).	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
What is microrheology?	A technique to measure viscoelastic properties of a material by tracking embedded tracer particles. Passive microrheology uses thermal motion only; active microrheology applies external forces (e.g. optical tweezers). Particularly useful for small sample volumes (e.g. inside cells).	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
State the generalised Stokes–Einstein relation linking ⟨r²(t)⟩ to G(ω).	For a viscous fluid: ⟨r²(t)⟩ = (k<sub>B</sub>T/(πaη))t<br><br>For a viscoelastic medium, the mean-square displacement of a tracer bead can be inverted (via Laplace transform) to obtain the frequency-dependent complex modulus G*(ω) — this is the basis of passive microrheology.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
In microrheology with the Maxwell model, what is ⟨r²(t)⟩?	⟨r²(t)⟩ = k<sub>B</sub>T/(πaG₀) + (k<sub>B</sub>T/(πaη))t<br><br>The first term is an elastic plateau at short times (particle confined by network); the second is linear diffusive growth at long times (particle escapes).	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
How can the shear modulus of a 3D network be estimated from its mesh size m?	G₀ ~ k<sub>B</sub>T/m³<br><br>One k<sub>B</sub>T per volume element of size m³. Each network strand contributes ~k<sub>B</sub>T to the elastic energy. Used for biopolymer gels.	soft_matter ch3_viscoelasticity
Define a polymer and a monomer.	A polymer is a long molecule formed by linking many identical or similar smaller units (monomers) via covalent bonds. The number of monomers N is the degree of polymerisation.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Define the number-average and weight-average molecular weights.	Number average: M<sub>n</sub> = ΣN<sub>i</sub>M<sub>i</sub> / ΣN<sub>i</sub><br>Weight average: M<sub>w</sub> = ΣN<sub>i</sub>M<sub>i</sub>² / ΣN<sub>i</sub>M<sub>i</sub><br><br>The polydispersity index PDI = M<sub>w</sub>/M<sub>n</sub> ≥ 1, with equality only for a perfectly monodisperse sample.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the end-to-end distance scaling for an ideal (random walk) chain.	⟨R²⟩ = Nb₀²<br><br>So R = √⟨R²⟩ = b₀√N. This is the freely jointed chain result. The exponent ν = 1/2.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
What is the freely jointed chain (FJC) model?	A polymer modelled as N rigid segments of length b₀, with each segment's orientation completely uncorrelated with the others. Equivalent to a 3D random walk with step length b₀.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Why does the freely rotating chain give ⟨R²⟩ = Nb₀²(1+cos θ)/(1−cos θ)?	The FJC overestimates flexibility because real polymer bonds have a fixed angle θ between successive segments. The correlation between consecutive segments is ⟨u<sub>i</sub>·u<sub>j</sub>⟩ = b₀²(cos θ)<sup>|i−j|</sup>, leading to a renormalised effective segment length.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Define the Kuhn length.	The effective segment length b such that the polymer can be described as an equivalent freely jointed chain with N<sub>K</sub> Kuhn segments. For the freely rotating chain: b = b₀(1+cos θ)/(1−cos θ). For a worm-like chain with persistence length l<sub>p</sub>: b = 2l<sub>p</sub>.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Define the persistence length l<sub>p</sub>.	The arc-length scale over which the tangent–tangent correlation along the polymer decays:<br><br>⟨t(s₁)·t(s₂)⟩ = exp(−|s₁−s₂|/l<sub>p</sub>)<br><br>It characterises the bending stiffness of a worm-like chain.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Relate the persistence length to bending rigidity for a worm-like chain.	l<sub>p</sub> = C<sub>B</sub>/(k<sub>B</sub>T)<br><br>where C<sub>B</sub> is the bending rigidity (elastic energy per unit length per unit curvature squared). Stiffer chains and lower temperatures give larger persistence lengths.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the end-to-end distance for a worm-like chain in two limits.	Long chain (L ≫ l<sub>p</sub>): ⟨R²⟩ = 2l<sub>p</sub>L (random walk with effective Kuhn length 2l<sub>p</sub>)<br><br>Short chain (L ≪ l<sub>p</sub>): ⟨R²⟩ = L² (chain is essentially rigid rod, fully extended)	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Give an example of a stiff biopolymer and its persistence length.	DNA: l<sub>p</sub> ≈ 50 nm (about 150 base pairs). Other examples include actin filaments (~17 μm) and microtubules (~1 mm). For comparison, flexible synthetic polymers have l<sub>p</sub> ~ 1 nm.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the probability distribution of the end-to-end vector for an ideal Gaussian chain.	P(R, N) = (3/(2πNb₀²))<sup>3/2</sup> exp(−3R²/(2Nb₀²))<br><br>A 3D Gaussian centred on the origin with variance ⟨R²⟩ = Nb₀².	soft_matter ch4_polymers
What is the entropy of an ideal Gaussian chain as a function of end-to-end distance?	S(R, N) = −k<sub>B</sub>(3/2)(R²/(Nb₀²)) + S(0)<br><br>The entropy decreases quadratically as the chain is stretched.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
What is the Helmholtz free energy of an ideal Gaussian chain?	F(R, N) = (3/2) k<sub>B</sub>T (R²/(Nb₀²)) + F(0)<br><br>This acts like a Hookean spring: the chain resists stretching with an entropic restoring force, with effective spring constant k = 3k<sub>B</sub>T/(Nb₀²).	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Why is polymer elasticity called 'entropic'?	The internal energy of an ideal chain is independent of conformation. The restoring force when the chain is stretched comes purely from the loss of entropy: stretched conformations have far fewer microstates than coiled ones. F = −TS, and stretching reduces S.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
What is excluded volume in a polymer?	The fact that two monomers cannot occupy the same physical location. This effectively gives a repulsive interaction between distant monomers along the chain that happen to be close in space, leading the chain to swell relative to the ideal random walk.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the Flory excluded volume free energy.	F<sub>excl</sub> = (3k<sub>B</sub>T v N²)/(4πR³)<br><br>where v is the excluded volume per segment. This is the entropic cost of avoiding overlap, proportional to N²/R³ (the number of pair contacts).	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Derive Flory's R ~ N^(3/5) result for a self-avoiding chain in good solvent.	Total free energy: F = (3k<sub>B</sub>T R²)/(2Nb₀²) + (3k<sub>B</sub>T v N²)/(4πR³).<br>Minimise dF/dR = 0:<br>R/(Nb₀²) = (9vN²)/(4πR⁴)<br>R⁵ ∝ vb₀²N³<br><br>R ~ N^(3/5)	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the radius scaling exponents for the three solvent regimes.	Good solvent (χ < 1/2): R ~ N^(3/5) (Flory exponent, swollen)<br>Theta solvent (χ = 1/2): R ~ N^(1/2) (ideal chain)<br>Poor solvent (χ > 1/2): R ~ N^(1/3) (collapsed globule, R³ ~ N)	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Define the Flory interaction parameter χ.	χ = (z/k<sub>B</sub>T)[ε<sub>ps</sub> − ½(ε<sub>pp</sub> + ε<sub>ss</sub>)]<br><br>where z is the lattice coordination number and ε<sub>ij</sub> are pair interaction energies. χ < 1/2 → good solvent; χ = 1/2 → theta; χ > 1/2 → poor solvent.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
How is the excluded volume parameter v(T) related to χ?	v(T) = b₀³(1 − 2χ)<br><br>v > 0 for χ < 1/2 (good solvent: monomers repel), v = 0 at the theta point, v < 0 for χ > 1/2 (poor solvent: monomers attract).	soft_matter ch4_polymers
What is the theta temperature?	The temperature at which the excluded volume parameter v(T) = 0, i.e. χ = 1/2. The chain behaves as an ideal random walk because attractive and repulsive contributions to the segment-segment interaction exactly cancel.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Describe the coil-globule transition.	At T > θ (good solvent), the polymer is a swollen coil with R ~ N^(3/5).<br>At T = θ, R ~ N^(1/2) (ideal).<br>At T < θ (poor solvent), the chain collapses into a compact globule with R ~ N^(1/3) due to attractive interactions dominating excluded volume repulsion.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the Flory–Huggins free energy of mixing for a polymer solution.	ΔF/(M k<sub>B</sub>T) = (Φ/N)ln Φ + (1−Φ)ln(1−Φ) + χΦ(1−Φ)<br><br>where M is the total number of lattice sites, Φ is the polymer volume fraction, N is the polymerisation number. The first two terms are translational entropy of polymer and solvent; the third is the enthalpy of mixing.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Why does the entropy of mixing for polymer solutions favour the solvent over the polymer?	Because the polymer entropy term is (Φ/N)ln Φ — the factor of 1/N reflects that N monomers must move together as a single chain. For long polymers (N large), this term is small. The solvent term (1−Φ)ln(1−Φ) dominates the entropic gain on mixing.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the osmotic pressure of a polymer solution from Flory–Huggins.	Π = (k<sub>B</sub>T/b₀³)[Φ/N + (1−2χ)Φ²/2 + ...]<br><br>The first term is the ideal van't Hoff law (small for large N); the second term is the virial correction with B₂ = (1−2χ)/2.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
What is the second virial coefficient B₂ for a polymer solution?	B₂ = (1 − 2χ)/2<br><br>Positive in good solvent (repulsive interactions), zero at the theta point, negative in poor solvent (attractive). It controls the thermodynamic stability of the solution.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Why do chains in a polymer melt behave ideally?	In a melt, each chain is surrounded by chains of identical chemistry. The excluded-volume repulsion that swells a chain in dilute solution is exactly counterbalanced by interactions with surrounding chains. Equivalently, χ = 0 and v = b₀³/N₂ → 0 for long surrounding chains. Result: R ~ N^(1/2) like an ideal chain.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Define the overlap concentration Φ*.	The polymer volume fraction at which the average concentration equals the concentration within an isolated coil. In good solvent:<br><br>Φ* ~ Nb₀³/R³ ~ N^(1−9/5) = N^(−4/5)<br><br>For larger N, the overlap concentration is lower.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
What are the dilute, semi-dilute, and concentrated regimes?	Dilute (Φ < Φ*): Coils isolated, no overlap.<br>Semi-dilute (Φ* < Φ ≪ 1): Coils overlap but solution mostly solvent. Chains form an entangled network.<br>Concentrated (Φ → 1): Dense polymer melt, strong overlap and screening.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the Flory–Huggins spinodal equation.	χ<sub>spinodal</sub>(Φ) = ½[1/(NΦ) + 1/(1−Φ)]<br><br>Obtained from ∂²f/∂Φ² = 0. The two-phase region lies above (or below in T-Φ) this curve; the homogeneous solution is unstable there.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Where is the critical point of a polymer solution?	Φ<sub>c</sub> = 1/(√N + 1) ≈ 1/√N (for large N)<br><br>χ<sub>c</sub> = ½(1 + 1/√N)² ≈ 1/2 + 1/√N<br><br>Note that χ<sub>c</sub> approaches 1/2 from above as N → ∞, and Φ<sub>c</sub> → 0 — the critical composition becomes very polymer-poor for long chains.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Define the grafting density of polymers on a surface.	σ = 1/D²<br><br>where D is the average distance between grafting points. For D > R (chain size): mushroom regime. For D < R: brush regime where chains stretch.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Derive the brush height in a polymer brush (good solvent).	Free energy per chain (chain in cylinder of radius D and height h):<br>F = (3k<sub>B</sub>T h²)/(2Nb₀²) + (k<sub>B</sub>T b₀³ N² σ)/h<br><br>Minimising dF/dh = 0:<br>h ~ b₀(b₀³σ)<sup>1/3</sup> N<br><br>Brush height is linear in N (unlike coiled chains).	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the shear modulus for a rubber (affine network model).	G = (n/V) k<sub>B</sub>T<br><br>where n/V is the number density of network strands (cross-link junctions). Note that rubber stiffens with increasing T — opposite to most materials — because the elasticity is entropic in origin.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Why does rubber elasticity increase with temperature?	Because rubber elasticity is entropic, not enthalpic. The restoring force comes from the loss of conformational entropy on stretching. At higher T, the entropic free energy contribution −TS is more important, so the restoring force is larger.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the Rouse equation for polymer dynamics.	γ(dr<sub>n</sub>/dt) = κ(r<sub>n+1</sub> + r<sub>n−1</sub> − 2r<sub>n</sub>) + ξ<sub>n</sub>(t)<br><br>Each bead has friction γ and is connected by harmonic springs of constant κ = 3k<sub>B</sub>T/b₀². Random forces ξ describe collisions with the solvent. Hydrodynamic interactions are neglected.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the Rouse time τ<sub>R</sub>.	τ<sub>R</sub> = γN²b₀²/(6π²k<sub>B</sub>T)<br><br>The longest relaxation time of the chain (time for the slowest internal mode, p=1, to relax). Scales as N², which is the time for the chain to diffuse over its own size.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the Rouse centre-of-mass diffusion coefficient.	D<sub>CM</sub> = k<sub>B</sub>T/(Nγ)<br><br>The whole chain has total friction Nγ and diffuses as a single object. Note D<sub>CM</sub> ∝ 1/N — slower for longer chains.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
What is the key difference between the Rouse and Zimm models?	Rouse model: hydrodynamic interactions neglected ('free-draining'). Each bead has independent friction. D ∝ 1/N.<br><br>Zimm model: HI included via the Oseen tensor. Solvent inside the coil is dragged with the chain. Effective friction is that of a sphere of radius R. D ∝ 1/(ηR) ∝ 1/N^ν.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the Zimm time τ<sub>Z</sub>.	τ<sub>Z</sub> ~ ηR³/k<sub>B</sub>T ~ τ₀ N^(3ν)<br><br>For an ideal chain (ν = 1/2): τ<sub>Z</sub> ~ N^(3/2). For good solvent (ν = 3/5): τ<sub>Z</sub> ~ N^(9/5). Slightly weaker N-dependence than the Rouse time τ<sub>R</sub> ~ N².	soft_matter ch4_polymers
What is reptation?	The motion of an entangled polymer chain through a tube formed by surrounding chains in a melt or gel. The chain cannot cross other chains so it must diffuse along its own contour, like a snake. Originally proposed by de Gennes/Edwards.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
State the reptation time τ<sub>rep</sub>.	τ<sub>rep</sub> ~ (ηb³/k<sub>B</sub>T) × N³/N<sub>e</sub><br><br>where N<sub>e</sub> is the entanglement length. The N³ scaling is much steeper than the Rouse N² because the chain must diffuse along its much longer contour length L ~ N b/√N<sub>e</sub>.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
How does the diffusion coefficient scale with N for a reptating chain?	D<sub>rep</sub> ~ (k<sub>B</sub>T/Nγ)(N<sub>e</sub>/N)² ∝ N⁻²<br><br>Much slower than Rouse (N⁻¹) or Zimm (N^−ν). Experimentally observed for entangled polymer melts.	soft_matter ch4_polymers
Define surface tension γ.	Energy per unit area required to create a new surface (units J/m² = N/m). Equivalently, the force per unit length acting tangential to a free surface that resists its expansion.	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
Give the thermodynamic definition of surface tension.	γ = (∂U/∂A)<sub>S,V,n</sub> = (∂F/∂A)<sub>T,V,n</sub> = (∂G/∂A)<sub>T,p,n</sub><br><br>It is the change in (internal/Helmholtz/Gibbs) free energy per unit increase in surface area, at fixed thermodynamic conditions.	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
Why do liquids form spherical droplets in the absence of gravity?	Because a sphere minimises the surface area for a given volume, which minimises the surface free energy γA. This is a direct consequence of surface tension and is observed for drops smaller than the capillary length.	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
State the Young–Laplace equation for the pressure jump across a curved interface.	Δp = γ(1/R₁ + 1/R₂) = 2γH<br><br>where R₁, R₂ are the principal radii of curvature and H = (1/R₁ + 1/R₂)/2 is the mean curvature. For a sphere of radius R: Δp = 2γ/R. For a soap bubble (two surfaces): Δp = 4γ/R.	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
Why is the pressure inside a soap bubble Δp = 4γ/R rather than 2γ/R?	Because a soap bubble has two interfaces (an inner and an outer surface of the soap film), each contributing 2γ/R to the pressure jump. A simple liquid droplet has only one interface, giving Δp = 2γ/R.	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
Define the capillary length l<sub>c</sub>.	l<sub>c</sub> = √(γ/(ρg))<br><br>The length scale below which surface tension dominates over gravity. For water: l<sub>c</sub> ≈ 2.7 mm. Drops smaller than l<sub>c</sub> are nearly spherical.	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
Derive the capillary rise height in a thin tube.	Hydrostatic pressure difference at height h: ρgh.<br>Laplace pressure across meniscus: 2γ/R (assuming spherical meniscus, R = tube radius for full wetting).<br>Equating: γ = ρghR/2 ⟹ h = 2γ/(ρgR).	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
State Young's equation for the contact angle.	γ<sub>SV</sub> = γ<sub>SL</sub> + γ<sub>LV</sub> cos θ<br><br>This is a force balance at the three-phase contact line: solid-vapour, solid-liquid, and liquid-vapour surface tensions. Solving for cos θ gives the equilibrium contact angle.	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
Define the wetting parameter S.	S = γ<sub>SV</sub> − (γ<sub>SL</sub> + γ<sub>LV</sub>)<br><br>S > 0: complete wetting (liquid spreads, θ = 0). S < 0: partial wetting (finite contact angle). S very negative: non-wetting (θ → 180°).	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
What is hydrophilic vs hydrophobic in terms of contact angle?	Hydrophilic surface: water has small contact angle θ < 90° (likes the surface).<br>Hydrophobic surface: water has large contact angle θ > 90° (avoids the surface).<br>Superhydrophobic: θ > 150°, achieved by surface roughness/pillars (e.g. lotus leaf).	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
Relate surface tension to the Hamaker constant.	γ = A/(24πa²)<br><br>where A is the Hamaker constant and a is half the molecular spacing. Comes from integrating the van der Waals attraction f(h) = A/(6πh³) between two halves of a liquid slab. Underestimates γ because dispersion is frequency-dependent.	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
State the Gibbs adsorption isotherm.	n/A = −(1/k<sub>B</sub>T) (dγ/d ln c)<br><br>The surface excess concentration n/A of solute at an interface is related to the rate at which surface tension γ changes with bulk solute concentration c. Surfactants reduce γ → positive n/A (surfactant accumulates at interface).	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
Why does surface tension stop decreasing above the CMC of a surfactant?	Above the critical micelle concentration, additional surfactant added to the solution goes into micelles rather than monomers. The chemical potential of monomers stops increasing, so the surface tension plateau is reached. This is often the simplest experimental method to measure CMC.	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
What is the Cahn–Hilliard model of an interface?	A free-energy functional approach that adds a gradient term penalising sharp concentration gradients:<br><br>F = ∫ dx [f(ϕ) + B(∇ϕ)²]<br><br>It allows the interface to have finite thickness ξ ~ √(B/A) rather than being a sharp discontinuity. Diverges at critical points.	soft_matter ch5_surfaces
Define a colloid (rough size criterion).	Particles or structures with at least one dimension in the range ~1 nm to ~1 μm — large enough that quantum effects are negligible, but small enough that thermal motion (k<sub>B</sub>T) significantly affects them. Examples: proteins, viruses, polymer latex, gold nanoparticles, smoke.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
What sets the upper size limit for colloids?	When ⟨h⟩ = k<sub>B</sub>T/(m<sub>b</sub>g) becomes smaller than the particle radius r, the particle sediments under gravity rather than diffusing. For typical density contrast (~1 g/cm³), this happens around r ≈ 1 μm.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
List the five main interactions between colloidal particles.	(1) Excluded volume (hard-core repulsion).<br>(2) Dispersion / van der Waals attraction.<br>(3) Coulomb repulsion (screened by electrolyte).<br>(4) Depletion (entropic attraction).<br>(5) Steric repulsion (e.g. polymer brush coating).	soft_matter ch6_colloids
State the van der Waals interaction between two atoms.	V(r) = −B/r⁶<br><br>where B = 2α²hν/(4πε₀)². This is the London dispersion force, of quantum mechanical origin (induced-dipole–induced-dipole interaction).	soft_matter ch6_colloids
State the vdW interaction between two parallel solid surfaces (per unit area).	V(h)/A = −A<sub>H</sub>/(12πh²)<br><br>where A<sub>H</sub> is the Hamaker constant (~10⁻²⁰ J typically) and h is the separation. Note the much slower decay (h⁻²) compared to atom-atom (r⁻⁶), because all pairs of atoms across the slabs contribute.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
State the vdW interaction between two spheres at close approach.	V<sub>sph-sph</sub>(h) ≈ −A<sub>H</sub>R/(12h)<br><br>where R is the sphere radius and h is the surface-to-surface separation (h ≪ R). Even slower decay than slab-slab — colloidal vdW interactions can be very long-ranged.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
Define the Bjerrum length.	l<sub>B</sub> = e²/(4πε₀ε<sub>r</sub>k<sub>B</sub>T)<br><br>The distance at which the Coulomb energy between two unit charges equals k<sub>B</sub>T. For water at 300 K (ε<sub>r</sub> ≈ 80), l<sub>B</sub> ≈ 0.7 nm. Independent of salt concentration.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
State the Poisson–Boltzmann equation.	∇²ϕ(x) = −(e/ε)Σ<sub>i</sub> z<sub>i</sub>n<sub>i,0</sub> exp(−z<sub>i</sub>eϕ/k<sub>B</sub>T)<br><br>Combines the Poisson equation with the Boltzmann distribution of ions in the local mean-field potential. Non-linear in general.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
State the Debye–Hückel (linearised PB) equation.	∇²ϕ(x) = κ²ϕ(x)<br><br>Obtained by Taylor-expanding the exponential in PB to first order, valid when eϕ ≪ k<sub>B</sub>T. The screening parameter κ = 1/λ.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
State the Debye–Hückel screening length.	λ = 1/κ = √(ε₀ε<sub>r</sub>k<sub>B</sub>T / (e²Σ<sub>i</sub>z<sub>i</sub>²n<sub>i,0</sub>))<br><br>For a 1:1 electrolyte: λ = √(ε₀ε<sub>r</sub>k<sub>B</sub>T / (2e²n₀)). The distance over which electrostatic potentials decay in an electrolyte solution.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
What is the Debye length of pure water at pH 7?	≈ 1 μm (≈ 960 nm)<br><br>Number density of H⁺ and OH⁻ ions is each 10⁻⁷ × N<sub>A</sub> × 1000 = 6.02 × 10¹⁹ m⁻³. The very low ion concentration in pure water gives a long screening length.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
What is the Debye length under physiological conditions (~0.1 M NaCl)?	≈ 1 nm<br><br>About 1000 times smaller than for pure water because the ion concentration is 10⁶ times larger (and λ ∝ 1/√n).	soft_matter ch6_colloids
Solve the PB equation for the potential near a single charged plane (low surface charge).	ϕ(x) = (σλ/ε<sub>w</sub>) exp(−x/λ)<br><br>The potential decays exponentially with the Debye length. Surface charge σ creates an electrostatic double layer of opposite sign in the solution.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
Define the Gouy–Chapman length.	λ<sub>GC</sub> = 2k<sub>B</sub>Tε<sub>w</sub>/(e|σ|)<br><br>The distance from a highly charged surface within which most counter-ions are concentrated. Smaller λ<sub>GC</sub> = stronger electrostatic interaction. Relevant when surface charges are large enough that DH linearisation breaks down.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
Describe the structure of an electric double layer at high surface charge.	Two regions: (1) a compact layer of counter-ions within ~λ<sub>GC</sub> of the surface, where the linearised theory fails because of high counter-ion density; and (2) a diffuse layer extending several Debye lengths λ where the Debye–Hückel description holds.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
What is DLVO theory?	Theory of colloidal stability combining attractive van der Waals interactions with repulsive screened-Coulomb interactions to predict the total interaction potential between two colloidal particles. Named after Derjaguin, Landau, Verwey, and Overbeek.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
State the screened Coulomb (DLVO repulsive) interaction between two charged spheres.	U<sub>e</sub>(r) = (Qe<sup>κR</sup>/(1+κR))² × e<sup>−κr</sup>/(ε₀ε<sub>r</sub>r)<br><br>where Q is the charge, R the radius, r the centre-to-centre separation, and κ = 1/λ.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
Sketch and explain the qualitative DLVO interaction profile.	Three features: (1) Deep primary minimum at very short range (vdW dominates) — aggregation. (2) Repulsive barrier at intermediate range (Coulomb dominates) — kinetic stabilisation. (3) Sometimes a shallow secondary minimum at larger range — metastable aggregation. High salt screens the repulsion → barrier disappears → particles aggregate.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
How does adding salt destabilise a colloidal suspension?	Increasing salt concentration decreases the Debye length λ, reducing the range of the repulsive Coulomb interaction. The DLVO barrier disappears, and the deep van der Waals primary minimum dominates → particles aggregate.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
What are depletion interactions?	An effective entropic attraction between large colloidal particles in a suspension containing smaller particles (or polymers). When the gap between two large colloids is smaller than the small particle diameter, small particles cannot enter; the imbalance in osmotic pressure pushes the large particles together.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
State the depletion interaction strength (square-well model).	U<sub>dep</sub> = −P<sub>osm</sub>V<sub>dep</sub><br><br>where P<sub>osm</sub> = (N/V)k<sub>B</sub>T is the osmotic pressure of the small particles and V<sub>dep</sub> is the overlap volume of the depletion zones. Range = small particle diameter; depth ~ a few k<sub>B</sub>T.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
How can polymer brushes stabilise colloids?	Steric stabilisation: when two particles coated with grafted polymer chains approach, the brushes overlap, compressing the chains and incurring an osmotic + elastic free energy cost. This creates a strong short-range repulsion that prevents direct contact and vdW-driven aggregation.	soft_matter ch6_colloids
List the three main driving forces for molecular self-assembly.	(1) Hydrogen bonding: directional, ~20 kJ/mol, important for protein folding and DNA.<br>(2) Hydrophobic effect: water expels non-polar solutes, drives micelle formation.<br>(3) Electrostatics (screened): can stabilise or destabilise depending on charge and ionic strength.	soft_matter ch7_selfassembly
Why is the hydrophobic effect entropically driven for small solutes?	Water can maintain its hydrogen-bonding network around a small (< ~1 nm) hydrophobe, but only by adopting a more constrained set of orientational microstates. This reduces water entropy. Aggregation of hydrophobes minimises the surface area of constrained water → restores entropy.	soft_matter ch7_selfassembly
How does the hydrophobic effect change for large solutes?	For solutes larger than ~1 nm, water cannot reorganise its H-bond network around them and must break hydrogen bonds at the surface. The cost becomes enthalpic rather than entropic (each broken H-bond is ~20 kJ/mol).	soft_matter ch7_selfassembly
Define the critical micelle concentration (CMC).	The concentration above which surfactant monomers preferentially form micelles rather than remain dissolved as monomers. Below the CMC: mostly monomers; above: monomer concentration ≈ CMC and excess surfactant goes into micelles.	soft_matter ch7_selfassembly
Express the CMC in terms of the energy gain Δε per amphiphile of joining a micelle.	c<sub>CMC</sub> ~ exp(Δε/k<sub>B</sub>T)<br><br>where Δε = ε<sub>α*</sub> − ε<sub>1</sub> is the difference in chemical potential per amphiphile between the micellar and monomer states. Larger driving force → smaller CMC.	soft_matter ch7_selfassembly
Define the surfactant packing parameter.	p = v/(a₀l<sub>c</sub>)<br><br>where v is the hydrocarbon tail volume, a₀ the head group area, and l<sub>c</sub> the maximum tail length. Determines the aggregate shape from geometry alone.	soft_matter ch7_selfassembly
Give the surfactant packing parameter ranges and corresponding shapes.	p < 1/3: spherical micelles<br>1/3 < p < 1/2: cylindrical micelles<br>1/2 < p < 1: bilayers and vesicles<br>p > 1: inverted micelles (e.g. in oil)	soft_matter ch7_selfassembly
Why must p < 1/3 for spherical micelles?	For spherical packing of α surfactants of volume v in a sphere of radius R = 3v/a₀, the constraint is R < l<sub>c</sub> (the tail must reach the centre). What are the main phases of self-assembled lipid systems with increasing water content?	Hexagonal (H, low water, tightly packed tubes), Lamellar (L, bilayers separated by water), Cubic (Q, water in voids), Vesicles (V, infinite dilution, ~99% water). Describe the three main phases of a lipid bilayer with temperature.	L<sub>β</sub> (gel/solid-ordered, lowest T): tilted tails, tightly packed, thin.<br>P<sub>β</sub> (ripple, intermediate T): undulating membrane, intermediate state.<br>L<sub>α</sub> (fluid, highest T): disordered tails, lipids freely mobile in the plane. How does pressure affect the lipid phase transition temperature?	ΔT<sub>m</sub>/T<sub>m</sub> = Δp ΔV/ΔH₀<br><br>Increasing pressure shifts T<sub>m</sub> to higher values (typically ~1 K per 40 bar for DMPC). State the Helfrich bending energy of a fluid membrane (per unit area).	f<sub>bend</sub> = ½κ(c₁ + c₂ − c₀)² + κ<sub>G</sub>c₁c₂<br><br>where κ is the bending rigidity, κ<sub>G</sub> the Gaussian rigidity, c₁, c₂ are principal curvatures, and c₀ is the spontaneous curvature.	soft_matter ch7_selfassembly
State the energy of a planar membrane subject to tension and bending.	δE = ∫dA [½σ|∇h|² + ½κ(∇²h)²]<br><br>For a Monge-parametrised membrane with height h(x,y), σ is the surface tension and κ the bending rigidity. State the spectrum of thermal membrane fluctuations.	⟨|h<sub>q</sub>|²⟩ = (k<sub>B</sub>T)/(A(σq² + κq⁴))<br><br>From equipartition applied to each Fourier mode. At long wavelengths (small q), tension dominates. At short wavelengths (large q), bending dominates.	soft_matter ch7_selfassembly
Why are membrane fluctuations dominated by long-wavelength modes?	Because the energy cost scales as σq² + κq⁴, fluctuations are largest at small q (long wavelengths) where the energy is smallest. ⟨|h<sub>q</sub>|²⟩ ∝ 1/q² for tension-dominated modes and 1/q⁴ for bending-dominated modes — both diverge at small q.	soft_matter ch7_selfassembly
Define the Helmholtz free energy of an α-cluster of surfactants.	F = Σ<sub>α</sub>[n<sub>α</sub>k<sub>B</sub>T(ln(n<sub>α</sub>Λ³<sub>α</sub>/V) − 1) + n<sub>α</sub>f<sub>α</sub>]<br><br>where n<sub>α</sub> is the number of clusters of size α, f<sub>α</sub> = −k<sub>B</sub>T ln z<sub>α</sub> is the internal free energy. At equilibrium, how is the concentration of α-clusters related to the monomer concentration?	c<sub>α</sub> = α[c₁ exp((ε₁ − ε<sub>α</sub>)/k<sub>B</sub>T)]<sup>α</sup><br><br>If ε<sub>α</sub> < ε₁ (aggregation favourable), large clusters dominate above the CMC. Equipartition theorem for a Brownian particle in a harmonic trap.	Each quadratic degree of freedom contributes ½k<sub>B</sub>T to the average energy. For a 3D harmonic trap V = ½kr², ⟨V⟩ = (3/2)k<sub>B</sub>T, so ⟨r²⟩ = 3k<sub>B</sub>T/k.	soft_matter formulas
What is the Smoluchowski diffusion-controlled rate?	k = 4πDa<br><br>The maximum rate of an irreversible bimolecular reaction in solution, limited only by diffusion to a reaction radius a. With D = k<sub>B</sub>T/(6πηa), this gives k ~ k<sub>B</sub>T/η, independent of size.	soft_matter formulas
Relation between Kuhn length and persistence length.	b<sub>Kuhn</sub> = 2l<sub>p</sub><br><br>For a worm-like chain. Number of Kuhn segments for a chain of contour length L.	N<sub>K</sub> = L/b<sub>Kuhn</sub> = L/(2l<sub>p</sub>)<br><br>Used to apply ideal-chain results ⟨R²⟩ = N<sub>K</sub>b² to a worm-like chain, giving ⟨R²⟩ = 2l<sub>p</sub>L for L ≫ l<sub>p</sub>.	soft_matter formulas
State the Cauchy stress relation.	dF<sub>i</sub> = σ<sub>ij</sub>n<sub>j</sub>dS<br><br>The force on a surface element dS with outward normal n is given by contracting the stress tensor with the normal vector. This is how stress and force are related on any surface.	soft_matter formulas
How does the persistence length depend on temperature for a worm-like chain?	l<sub>p</sub> = C<sub>B</sub>/(k<sub>B</sub>T) ∝ 1/T<br><br>Higher temperature → smaller persistence length (chain bends more easily). Mean free path of momentum diffusion in time t.	δ ~ √(νt) = √((η/ρ)t)<br><br>The distance momentum diffuses through a fluid of kinematic viscosity ν = η/ρ in time t. Same form as Einstein diffusion of particles.	soft_matter formulas
Time for a colloid to diffuse its own diameter.	τ<sub>r</sub> = r²/D = 6πηr³/(k<sub>B</sub>T)<br><br>Combining ⟨x²⟩ = 6Dt with the Stokes–Einstein relation. For r = 1 μm in water, τ<sub>r</sub> ~ 1 s.	soft_matter formulas
Why is rubber elasticity entropic?	Because the internal energy of an ideal Gaussian chain is independent of conformation — there is no enthalpic preference for stretched vs coiled states. The free energy F = U − TS is dominated by −TS, and stretching reduces S because there are far fewer stretched conformations than coiled ones.	soft_matter formulas
Why does water wet a clean glass surface?	Glass is hydrophilic (high surface energy γ<sub>SV</sub>).
